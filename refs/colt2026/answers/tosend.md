We thank the reviewers for the reading and comments. We respond point by point below.

1) Reviewer 1
We agree that the motivation for deep kernels should be made clearer. Our goal is not to claim that deep RF-LR kernels are always preferable, nor to argue against feature learning. Rather, the NTK viewpoint gives access to quantities governing optimization in the lazy regime, such as PL-type bounds, smallest-eigenvalue bounds, and condition-number bounds for the Gram matrix; see, e.g., 2405.14630 on pyramidal networks and 2402.01868 on PL-based convergence arguments. The point is to analyze how depth affects conditioning while keeping a tractable kernel description.

This matters because finite-width corrections for full-rank networks are hard: perturbative / field-theoretic calculations quickly become complicated and rely on Wick / Isserlis expansions. By contrast, the low-rank bottleneck gives a simpler structure for correlations. We view the paper as a first step toward a systematic analysis of finite-width corrections and their optimization consequences. This is also motivated by recent finite-width work suggesting nontrivial depth-dependent scaling laws for NTK corrections (e.g. 2508.11522), where finer analysis could eventually yield optimization bounds.

We will also clarify the meaning of the "benefit" of depth in the kernel regime. For very deep full-rank NTKs, prior work such as terjek2025mlpsateoc indicates depth-dependent spectral effects, but the stable discrete-time learning rate may become very small since it scales like 1/lambda_max. Our point is not that inverse-depth scaling is impossible, but that the resulting conditioning tradeoff matters. In practice, extremely deep moderate-width networks can be difficult to tune; see 2503.14858 on 1000-layer self-supervised RL. Related optimal-scaling results give 1/sqrt(L), so one trades optimization guarantees for slower training; one also sees 1/sqrt(width)-type behavior in very deep infinite-width analyses. In our setting, lower rank and larger depth give a controlled tradeoff under fixed budget, and the condition number is the right object, not only lambda_min.

Regarding the choice to train A^(l) and c^(l) while freezing w^(l), there are four reasons. First, previous RF-LR analyses (e.g. 2407.00765 (RF-LR)) indicate that fixing the feature directions already reduces cost while preserving the kernel-regime picture. Second, if w^(l) were also trained, one enters a less stable regime where one must control how much those directions move, or else switch to Riemannian optimization on low-rank manifolds. Third, training w^(l) also brings in a Barron / mean-field interpretation that belongs to a different analysis involving Wasserstein gradient flow, muP / Yang-Hayou scaling, or local-feature regimes in the spirit of 2603.18168, left for future work. Fourth, there is empirical evidence that low-rank networks and LoRA-type parameterizations can have a favorable optimization bias (e.g. recent LoRA analyses such as arXiv:2402.16842). Freezing w^(l) therefore isolates r as the key control parameter and yields the tractable setting in which our recursion and finite-width questions are analyzable.

3) Reviewer 3
We agree that the paper should better emphasize why RF-LR is an interesting NTK object. Our claim is not that NTK theory explains all practical uses of RF-LR beyond the lazy regime. Rather, we study RF-LR because it provides a low-rank lens on NTK questions that are hard in the full-rank setting, especially finite-width corrections, depth-dependent conditioning, and Gram spectra. The paper gives a fresh low-rank view on NTK guarantees developed from 2019 to 2026 for full-rank networks, at the cost of a probabilistic analysis of the NTK Gram spectrum.

The low-rank bottleneck also gives an interpretable family of partial functions f_r, and this is something we want to emphasize much more clearly. Our point is that what is often called "feature learning" is usually phrased in weight space, whereas here one can describe a function-space notion of local feature movement: if a bottleneck partial function f_r(x) moves nontrivially by aggregating many small parameter changes, then something meaningful is being learned even in a regime where the NTK description remains relevant. This is close in spirit to constructing observable low-dimensional quantities from wide networks, whereas in mean-field work such observables often come from Wasserstein shifts of the parameter distribution. Here the bottleneck functions give another family of observables while also reducing the number of neurons. Beyond the NTK regime, there is empirical motivation as well; for example, 2407.00765 show strong performance on highly oscillatory targets. Here we deliberately focus on the kernel regime where rigorous analysis is possible.

Finally, on the phrase "depth and rank commute": we agree that the wording can be confusing. Our intended meaning is not that depth and rank are literally interchangeable. Rather, in the NTK regime the limiting kernel function may stay in the same RKHS class, while depth and rank affect optimization through conditioning and finite-width fluctuations, not through the spherical-harmonic decomposition of the limiting zonal kernel itself. We will rephrase this more carefully. More broadly, finite-width corrections are a major open problem: in the full-rank case they are technically involved, whereas RF-LR seems to offer a simpler route. Very deep NTKs are still poorly understood except in simplified settings (e.g. 2202.02981), and phenomena such as neural collapse or low-rank bias in training may be relevant here; see also recent work by 2505.21722 In that sense, the present paper aims to lay the groundwork for a more tractable finite-width theory for deep low-rank networks. We believe this is a important open direction.
