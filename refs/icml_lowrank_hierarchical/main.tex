\begin{abstract}
We provide experimental evidence that low-rank neural networks construct features hierarchically through sequential activation of frequency bands, while networks without low-rank constraints (or with rank too high) fail to build meaningful features. Our analysis reveals a fundamental dichotomy: in the extensive-rank regime ($r \asymp d^\beta$ for $\beta \in (0,1)$), training exhibits stepwise loss curves corresponding to hierarchical feature construction via band-by-band Fourier mode activation. In contrast, when rank is too high ($r \asymp d$ or full-rank), the network fails to develop hierarchical structure and instead learns flat, non-hierarchical representations. We connect this phenomenon to mean-field dynamics and show that instability near sharp minima acts as a reinforcement mechanism, driving the hierarchical construction process. Our experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets confirm that low-rank is not merely a parameter-efficient approximation, but a fundamental architectural choice that enables hierarchical feature learning.
\end{abstract}
