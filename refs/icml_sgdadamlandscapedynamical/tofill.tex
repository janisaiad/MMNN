\begin{abstract}

This work studies the training dynamics of low-rank neural networks with frozen random features in the mean-field regime.
Convergence to a global minimizer is established for gradient-based training under standard independent and identically distributed initialization, despite the presence of low-rank constraints and nonconvex loss functions.
By explicitly incorporating low-rank structure into the network architecture, a tractable mean-field evolution system is derived. Its well-posedness is established, and it is shown that alternating trained and frozen layers preserves the universal approximation property while simplifying the learning dynamics.
The analysis identifies a rank-channel feature learning mechanism, in which different low-rank channels specialize to distinct spatial locations and progressively capture higher-frequency components. This mechanism explains both the persistence of global convergence and the emergence of hierarchical frequency learning.
Numerical experiments demonstrate that low-rank networks achieve faster convergence and higher accuracy on highly oscillatory targets, while using substantially fewer parameters than full-rank networks.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Neural networks have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of applications, including computer vision, natural language processing, scientific computing, and data-driven modeling. These advances have reshaped modern machine learning practice and enabled solutions to problems that were previously considered intractable. Despite this empirical success, theoretical understanding of neural network training remains limited.
A central difficulty lies in the highly non-convex nature of the learning landscape. For most architectures and problem settings, the optimization process is poorly understood: it is generally unclear why gradient-based methods succeed in practice, how representations evolve during training, or under what conditions convergence can be guaranteed. As a result, much of current understanding relies on empirical observations rather than rigorous analysis.

Recent progress in mean-field theory has begun to address this gap. In particular, convergence to a global minimizer has been established for full-rank three-layer neural networks without assuming convexity of the loss function \cite{pham2021globalconvergencethreelayerneural}. This result demonstrates that, for certain architectures, global convergence can occur despite severe non-convexity, providing a rare and valuable theoretical explanation for successful training.

However, full-rank models are heavily over-parameterized. The number of trainable parameters and the associated computational cost can be far larger than what is effectively needed to represent the target function. From both computational and modeling perspectives, it is natural to seek more structured and efficient alternatives. Low-rank neural networks offer such a possibility by reducing parameter redundancy and computation while retaining expressive power.
This leads to a fundamental question: does global convergence persist under low-rank constraints?
More specifically, when full-rank weight matrices are replaced by low-rank factorizations, can gradient-based training still be guaranteed to converge to a global minimizer, or is full rank essential for existing convergence results?

Low-rank networks factorize weight matrices into products of tall, skinny matrices (for example, $W = LR^{\top}$ with rank $r \ll \min\{n,m\}$), substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters.  Yet, from a theoretical perspective, it remains unclear whether the favorable optimization properties observed in full-rank mean-field analyses extend to this low-rank setting.

These considerations motivate a systematic theoretical investigation of
low-rank neural networks from the perspectives of optimization and
representation learning. The central objective is to determine whether the
favorable global convergence properties recently established for full-rank
mean-field models persist under low-rank constraints, and, if so, how the
resulting representations are structured at convergence.
This question is resolved in the present work, as summarized by the following
informal theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence to a global minimizer for any depth with i.i.d. initialization (informal)]
    \label{thm:convergence-informal}
    For any depth $L \ge 2$, low-rank random feature networks with standard i.i.d. initialization only converges to a global minimizer of the population loss.
    \end{theorem}
    

Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence-informal} is an informal statement of the main
convergence result proved rigorously in
Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}. Beyond global convergence, the analysis further
establishes a hierarchical frequency learning phenomenon for low-rank neural
networks, revealing how learned representations are organized across different
low-rank channels.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{4pt}
	\item \textbf{Global convergence under low-rank constraints}
	(Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}).  
	It is shown that low-rank random feature neural networks converge
	exclusively to global minimizers of the population loss for any depth
	$L \ge 2$ under standard independent and identically distributed
	initialization.
	Unlike existing full-rank analyses, the result requires no special or
	ad-hoc initialization.

	\item \textbf{Channel-wise feature learning mechanism}
	(Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main}).  
	A theoretical characterization of feature organization at convergence
	is established for low-rank networks.
	A rigorously analyzed toy model demonstrates that each low-rank channel
	learns a spatially localized feature at a \emph{distinct location}, while
	channels separate hierarchically across \emph{frequency}.
	Lower-frequency components are captured first, followed by progressively
	higher-frequency components, yielding representations that are both
	spatially localized and frequency-separated.
\end{itemize}

Numerical experiments confirm that this theoretical structure translates into
practical benefits.
On frequency-dependent function approximation tasks, low-rank networks achieve
substantially faster convergence and significantly higher accuracy, reaching
relative mean squared errors on the order of $10^{-6}$ for highly oscillatory
targets, compared to approximately $10^{-3}$ for fully connected networks.
At the same time, the number of trainable parameters is reduced by
$95\%$--$99\%$ relative to full-rank models.
These results suggest a fundamental insight into the loss landscape:
appropriately structured low-rank parameterizations can improve optimization
behavior, rather than merely restricting expressivity.




A key theoretical advance of this work is the elimination of the ad-hoc
initialization assumptions that restrict existing full-rank multi-layer
convergence results to depth $L=3$ \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}.
In the low-rank random feature setting, frozen random features ensure that
$\operatorname{supp}(L^0(C_1)) = \mathbb{R}^d$ is preserved throughout training.
As a consequence, standard independent and identically distributed
initialization suffices, and convergence to a global minimizer holds for
\emph{arbitrary depth} $L \ge 2$.

Beyond global convergence, the theory further reveals a
\emph{channel-wise feature learning mechanism}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main}) that explains how low-rank
channels organize representations at convergence.
In particular, each channel becomes spatially localized, learning a spike
centered at a distinct spatial location,
This yields representations that are simultaneously spatially localized and
frequency-separated across the $r$ low-rank channels.

\subsection{Mean-Field Theory for Neural Networks}

Mean-field theory rigorously analyzes neural network training dynamics in the large-width limit (typically $N \ge 1000$ neurons per layer). In this regime, the empirical distribution of parameters evolves deterministically, leading to powerful tools for understanding optimization and generalization. Foundational results by \cite{mei2018mean}, \cite{chizat2018lazy}, \cite{javanmard2019mean}, \cite{rotskoff2019neural}, and \cite{wei2019regularization} established global convergence for two-layer networks under convex loss assumptions—crucially leveraging convexity to guarantee that all stationary points are global minimizers.

Progress beyond the convex case has accelerated. \cite{pham2021globalconvergencethreelayerneural} proved global convergence for three-layer networks without assuming convex loss functions. Mean-field analyses of residual networks \cite{lu2020mean} and multilayer networks \cite{sirignano2019mean} further clarify when global optima arise from stationary points, but these results so far apply only to full-rank networks. Whether such convergence persists in computationally efficient, low-rank architectures remained open.

We address this gap: we avoid Riemannian gradient descent (RGD) by training only the right factor $R$, keeping the left factor $L$ frozen as random features. by leveraging frozen random features, we prove that channel feature learning (in which $r$ independent channels capture hierarchical frequency structure) leads to both global convergence and practical representational learning.







The present analysis is closely related to the mean-field framework of
Chizat and Bach \cite{chizat2018global}, who established an informal global
optimality result (Theorem~2) for two-layer networks.
Specifically, if the support of the initial distribution spans all directions
in $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ and the feature map $\Psi$ is positively $2$-homogeneous,
then any weak limit of the Wasserstein gradient flow must correspond to a
global minimizer of the population risk.
The results developed here extend this principle to \emph{low-rank,
multi-layer} neural networks.

The analysis adapts mean-field techniques for random feature models, building
on the convergence theory developed in
\cite{pham2021globalconvergencethreelayerneural,nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}.
The introduction of frozen random features allows the removal of the ad-hoc
initialization conditions imposed in
\cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}; see
Remark~\ref{rem:correlated-init} for a detailed discussion.
For a comprehensive account of the full-rank setting, the reader is referred
to \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}.






\subsection{Low-Rank Neural Networks}

Our RF-LR architecture is based on \cite{zhang2025structuredbalancedmulticomponentmultilayer}. Most theoretical work on low-rank networks focuses on expressivity and approximation power, while their training dynamics—especially regarding convergence—have received less attention. \cite{kamalakara2022exploringlowranktraining} study end-to-end low-rank training, often using reparameterization gradient descent (RGD); in contrast, we train only the post-activation parameters, keeping the other factor as frozen random features. This architectural choice enables us to rigorously prove global convergence for low-rank networks and to elucidate the mechanism by which channel feature learning provides both theoretical and empirical benefits: each channel evolves largely independently, specializing to distinct spatial and frequency patterns.

\subsection{Random Features and Neural Tangent Kernel}

Random features provide a bridge between neural networks and kernel methods. In the mean-field width limit, fixing the first layer as random features connects the network to kernel methods governed by the neural tangent kernel (NTK) \cite{jacot2018neural}, offering tractable analyses of optimization and generalization. Our architecture embraces this by freezing the first-layer weights, so that low-rank mixing layers can learn task-specific representations atop a fixed random feature basis. Recent work establishes that low-rank structure suffices for the MLP NTK \cite{author2024lowrankntk}, while our results extend this to mean-field dynamics and global convergence. Together, these advances show that, both in NTK and mean-field regimes, low-rank networks can match the optimization guarantees of their full-rank counterparts.

\subsection{Low-Rank Random Feature Architecture}
\label{sec:setup}

We consider a low-rank random feature (RF-LR) network architecture. Let $h^{(0)}(x) = x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$. For layers $\ell = 1, \ldots, L$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:RF-LR}
h^{(\ell)}(x) = \frac{1}{n_\ell} \sum_{j=1}^{n_\ell} w^{(\ell)}_j\,\varphi_\ell\!\left(L^{(\ell)\top}_j\,h^{(\ell-1)}(x) + b^{(\ell)}_j\right) + c_{\ell},
\end{equation}
where $n_\ell$ is the width of layer $\ell$, $w^{(\ell)}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\ell}$ are trainable coefficient vectors, $L^{(\ell)}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell-1}}$ and $b^{(\ell)}_j \in \mathbb{R}$ are frozen random features (i.i.d. uniform or Gaussian), and $c$ is a trainable scalar output bias. The $1/n_\ell$ scaling ensures a well-defined mean-field width limit. In the mean-field width limit (typically $N \ge 1000$ neurons per layer), where $N$ denotes the width of each layer, under the mean-field parameterization with i.i.d. initialization (uniform or Gaussian), the empirical distribution of weights converges to a deterministic measure-valued evolution.

\textbf{Training policy:} $w^{(\ell)}$ and $c_{\ell}$ are trained; $L^{(\ell)}$ and $b^{(\ell)}$ are frozen random draws. In the mean-field formulation, biases $b^{(\ell)}_j$ are encoded by augmenting the input data with a constant component (adding 1 to the input vector).

\subsection{Mean-Field Forward Equations}
\label{subsec:forward-equations}

We use the \emph{neuronal embedding} framework of \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}: each neuron is indexed by a label $C$ lying in a probability space (countable or uncountable), which we specify when sampling or constructing the network. In the finite-width case, $C$ corresponds to discrete neuron indices; in the mean-field limit, $C$ runs over the support of a measure, and expectations $\E_C[\cdot]$ replace empirical averages over neurons.

\paragraph{Definition.}
We define the mean-field forward pass layer by layer. The \emph{frozen} first-layer feature map is $L^0(c_1) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$; we specify the law of $L^0(C_1)$ when sampling the network. In layers $i \ge 2$, the low-rank structure is given by a \emph{frozen} mixing matrix $L$ (entries $L_{c_2,k}$, or $L^{(i)}_{c_i,k}$ for intermediate layers), also fixed random features drawn at initialization; we call it \emph{mixing} in those layers. All other layer-wise quantities are defined exactly as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Layer $i=0$ (input):} $h^0(X)=X \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$. No neuronal index.
\item \textbf{Layer $i=1$ (first hidden):} Neuronal index $C_1 \in \mathcal{C}_1$. Frozen feature map $\underbrace{L^0(c_1) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}}_{\mathclap{L^{(1)}_j,\,b^{(1)}_j}}$. Channel-wise partial functions $f_k$, $k \in \{1,\ldots,r\}$:
  \[
  f_k(t;X,W) = \E_{C_1}\Bigl[ \underbrace{w_1(t,C_1,k)}_{\mathclap{w^{(1)}_j\ \text{(train.)}}} \,\varphi_1\bigl( \underbrace{\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X}}_{\mathclap{a_1(c_1;X),\ \text{pre-act.}}} \bigr) \Bigr],
  \]
  with activation $\varphi_1(a_1)$.
\item \textbf{Layer $i=2$ (second hidden):} Neuronal index $C_2 \in \mathcal{C}_2$. Pre-activation and activation:
  \[
  \underbrace{H_2(t,c_2;X,W)}_{\mathclap{\text{pre-act.}}} = \sum_{k=1}^r \underbrace{L_{c_2,k}}_{\mathclap{\text{mixing}}}\, \underbrace{f_k(t;X,W)}_{\mathclap{\text{channel}}}, \quad \underbrace{\varphi_2(H_2)}_{\mathclap{\text{activation}}}.
  \]
\item \textbf{Layer $i=3$ (output):} Network output:
  \[
  \hat{y}(X;W(t)) = \E_{C_2}\Bigl[ \underbrace{w_2(t,C_2)}_{\mathclap{w^{(3)}_j\ \text{(train.)}}} \,\underbrace{\varphi_2\bigl(H_2(t,C_2;X,W(t))\bigr)}_{\mathclap{\text{activation}}} \Bigr].
  \]
\item \textbf{Layers $i \in \{2,\ldots,L-1\}$ (intermediate, $L>3$):} Pre-activation
  \[
  H_i(t,c_i;X,W) = \sum_{k=1}^r \underbrace{L^{(i)}_{c_i,k}}_{\mathclap{\text{mixing} i}}\, \underbrace{f_k^{(i)}(t;X,W)}_{\mathclap{\text{channel, layer } i}},
  \]
  where
  \[
  \begin{split}
  f_k^{(i)}(t;X,W) &= \E_{C_{i-1}}\!\bigl[w_{i-1}(t,C_{i-1},k)\\
  &\quad\,\varphi_{i-1}(H_{i-1}(t,C_{i-1};X,W))\bigr],
  \end{split}
  \]
  with $H_{i-1}$ the pre-activation at layer $i-1$.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Mean-Field Backward Equations (ODEs)}
\label{sec:derivation}

The mean-field ODE system (backward equations) for the weights takes the form:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\partial_t w_1(t,c_1,k) &= -\xi_1(t)\,\E_Z[d_L\,\varphi_1(\ip{L^0}{X})\,B_k], \\
\partial_t w_2(t,c_2) &= -\xi_2(t)\,\E_Z[d_L\,\varphi_2(H_2)],
\end{split}
\label{eq:ode-w1}
\end{equation}
where $\E_Z[\cdot]$ denotes expectation over $Z=(X,Y)$; $d_L(Z;W(t)) = \partial_{\hat{y}} \mathscr{L}(Y, \hat{y}(X;W(t)))$ is the loss derivative; $\xi_1(t), \xi_2(t) \ge 0$ are the learning-rate schedules for the first and second layers. The channel-wise partial functions and backpropagated signal are
\begin{align*}
f_k(t;X,W) &= \E_{C_1}\big[w_1(t,C_1,k)\,\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})\big], \\
B_k(t;X) &= \E_{C_2}\big[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t,C_2;X,W))\,w_2(t,C_2)\big]
\end{align*}
(ReLU: $\relugate$).
















\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

The applicability of our results depends crucially on the following assumptions, which we state explicitly in Appendix~\ref{app:assumptions}. These assumptions are the core of the theoretical framework and determine when convergence to a global minimizer can be guaranteed and are \textbf{satisfied by standard MLP architectures}

The main assumptions are: \emph{Bounded Activations and Mixing} (Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}), \emph{Sub-Gaussian Initialization} (Assumption~\ref{assump:init}), \emph{Data Distribution and Loss Regularity} (Assumption~\ref{assump:data}), \emph{Diversity of Random Features} (Assumption~\ref{assump:diversity}), \emph{Non-Degeneracy} (Assumption~\ref{assump:lr}), and \emph{Training convergence to limit point} (Assumption~\ref{assump:convergence}). The \emph{Non-Degeneracy} assumption requires that the initial loss is better than the trivial zero predictor, ensuring the network learns a non-trivial solution. The \emph{Convergence to Limit Point} assumption states that training reaches a limit point, which is a natural condition for analyzing convergence.

\textbf{Universal approximation is automatically maintained:} Frozen random features with full support $\text{supp}(L^0(C_1)) = \mathbb{R}^d$ ensure that $\{\text{ReLU}(\langle L^0(c_1), \cdot\rangle) : c_1 \in \Omega_1\}$ has dense span in $L^2(\mathcal{P}_X)$, since $\text{ReLU}(af+b)$ for random $a,b$ always forms a dense span. This property is maintained throughout training because $L^0(C_1)$ are frozen.

These assumptions are satisfied by ReLU/SwiGLU networks on bounded data with standard initialization (Gaussian/Xavier).

\subsection{Well-Posedness: Existence and Uniqueness}
\label{sec:wellposed}

We establish well-posedness of the mean-field ODE system \eqref{eq:ode-w1} by adapting the proof from \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield} to account for the low-rank structure.

\begin{theorem}[Well-posedness of mean-field ODEs]
\label{thm:wellposed}
Under \emph{Bounded Activations and Mixing} (Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}), \emph{Sub-Gaussian Initialization} (Assumption~\ref{assump:init}), \emph{Data Distribution and Loss Regularity} (Assumption~\ref{assump:data}), and \emph{Diversity of Random Features} (Assumption~\ref{assump:diversity}), there exists a unique solution to the mean-field ODE system \eqref{eq:ode-w1} on $t\in[0,\infty)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We adapt introduce weight-space Orlicz sub-gaussian norms accounting for the $r$ channels ($\max_{1 \le k \le r}$ over channel index $k$, see Appendix~\ref{app:wellposed-norms}), establish sub-Gaussian a priori bounds with $r$-factors, then show the solution operator $F$ is contractive in these spaces; Banach fixed point yields existence and uniqueness. Details in Appendix~\ref{app:wellposed-proof}.
\end{proof}




\subsection{Global convergence}
\label{sec:convergence}


\begin{theorem}[RF-LR training only converges to global minimizers ]
\label{thm:convergence}
Under all assumptions from \emph{Bounded Activations and Mixing} (Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}) through \emph{Convergence to Limit Point} (Assumption~\ref{assump:convergence}), the mean-field dynamics for low-rank random feature networks converge to a global minimizer of the loss function: $\lim_{t\to\infty} W(t)=W^*$ where $W^*$ minimizes the population loss. For any depth $L \ge 2$, this holds with standard i.i.d initialization.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Proof strategy:} We extend \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}; the low-rank structure yields $r$ channels via $L$, and frozen random features preserve universal approximation, so convergence to a global minimizer holds for any depth with standard i.i.d. initialization. Full-rank arguments extend without ad-hoc init (Remark~\ref{rem:correlated-init}).

\begin{remark}[Avoiding ad-hoc initialization]
\label{rem:correlated-init}
Full-rank multilayer nets ($L \geq 3$) need \emph{ad-hoc initialization} in that framework: otherwise intermediate layers collapse and diversity fails (training can collapse the required full support). We avoid this: frozen features keep $\text{supp}(L^0(C_1)) = \mathbb{R}^d$, so standard i.i.d.\ initialization suffices and convergence to a global minimizer holds for any $L$.
\end{remark}

















\subsection{Quantitative Guarantees}
\label{sec:rate}

We provide a quantitative approximation theorem that bounds the error between finite-width networks and the mean-field limit. The detailed theorem and proof are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:quantitative}; here we summarize the key result.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-width approximation error bound]
\label{thm:quantitative-main}
Given a family $\mathsf{Init}$ of initialization laws and a tuple $\{n_1, n_2\}$ that is in the index set of $\mathsf{Init}$, perform the coupling procedure for the low-rank architecture as described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Fix a terminal time $T \in \epsilon\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}$. Under Assumptions~\ref{assump:regularity}, \ref{assump:quant-init} (see Appendix~\ref{app:quantitative}), and the low-rank structure with mixing matrix $L$ satisfying $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$, for $\epsilon \le 1$, we have with probability at least $1-2\delta$,
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le C_{\exp} \cdot C_{\text{width}} \cdot C_{\log},
\]
where $C_{\exp} = e^{K_T(1+rK)}$, $C_{\text{width}} = 1/\sqrt{n_{\min}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}$, $C_{\log} = \sqrt{\log(3(T+1)n_{\max}^2/\delta + e)}$, with $n_{\min} = \min\{n_1, n_2\}$, $n_{\max} = \max\{n_1, n_2\}$, $K_T = K(1+T^K)$, and the factor $(1+rK)$ accounts for the low-rank structure through $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$.

The exponential factor $e^{K_T(1+rK)}$ reflects the channel feature learning structure of low-rank networks: the $r$ channels evolve independently through the mixing matrix $L$, and the worst-case time complexity becomes exponential in $rK$ when all channels must be learned simultaneously. However, channel feature learning enables a \emph{choice} of which channels to activate, and in practice, only a subset of channels are needed, avoiding the worst-case exponential scaling.
\end{theorem}

The theorem links $\mathbf{W}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor)$ (discrete finite-width) to $W(t)$ (mean-field limit): error $O(1/\sqrt{n_{\min}}+\sqrt{\epsilon})$, independent of $d$. Versus full-rank \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield}, the exponential is $e^{K_T(1+rK)}$ instead of $e^{K_T}$ because the $r$ channels evolve through $L$ ($\|L\|_{\infty,1}\le rK$); effective capacity is constrained by $r$. Proof in Appendix~\ref{app:quantitative}.

\subsection{Mechanism of Spike Learning}
\label{rem:dominance-mechanism}
The log-ratio growth in Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main} arises from a self-reinforcing dominance loop. The evolution $\partial_t f_k(t,x)=-\xi_1(t)\,\E_{Z=(X,Y)}[d_L(Z;W(t))\,B_k(t;X)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,X)]$ is an integral over the data distribution, where $k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}$ is the channel index and $K_{\mu_0}(x,X)$ is the kernel. When channel $k$ dominates at $x_0$,
\[
H_2 = \sum_{j=1}^r L_{c_2,j}f_j = \underbrace{L_{c_2,k}f_k}_{\text{large}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j \neq k} L_{c_2,j}f_j}_{\text{small}},
\]
so the pre-activation and hence the gate $\one\{H_2>0\}$ are effectively driven by that channel. The backpropagated signal $B_k(t;X)=\E_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,w_2(t,C_2)\,\one \{H_2(t,C_2;X)>0\}]$ is a gated mixture over the second layer. The precise signs of $L_{c_2,k}$ or $w_2(t,c_2)$ need not matter: once a channel dominates, the ReLU gate selects a half-space (e.g.\ $\one \{L_{C_2,j}>0\}$), $w_2$ increases on that set via the mean-field $w_2$-ODE, and the dynamics create an \emph{emergent} sign-coherence rather than requiring it a priori. If the activation (gate) is correlated with a channel, that correlation \emph{amplifies}: the channel gets a larger $B_k$, hence a larger $\partial_t f_k$, so the channel and the gate become even more correlated. Under sign-coherence ($B_{k,1}$ has the same sign as $f_k$), the integral contribution from the dominant channel is larger, yielding a single self-reinforcing loop: larger dominance $\to$ larger $B_k$ $\to$ larger $\partial_t f_k$ $\to$ further amplified dominance. These mechanisms are proven in the two-sided step toy model (Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main}); see Appendix~\ref{app:r2-logratio-proof}.





\subsection{Toy model with provable spike feature learning}
\label{sec:fourier-toy}

The full mean-field system for discrete regression in Spatial-Fourier space is infinite dimensional; the two-point ($m=2$) dynamics nonetheless capture the essential mechanism for feature learning.
We state and prove the main result for the \emph{two-sided step} toy model: $m=2$ support points $x^{(1)}=x_0=-\delta$, $x^{(2)}=x_1=+\delta$ ($\delta>0$) with $y^{(1)}=+A$, $y^{(2)}=-A$. The target $y(x)$ is supported on $\{x_0,x_1\}$ with values $\pm A$ (Figure~\ref{fig:twostep}). For finitely-supported data, the $w_1$-evolution is linear in feature space once residuals and backprop signals are fixed; one obtains closed-form $f_k$ as a superposition of kernel bumps. Define $d_p(t)=d_L((x^{(p)},y^{(p)});W(t))$, $B_{k,p}(t)=B_k(t;x^{(p)})$, and
\[
\Gamma_{k,p}(t) \equiv \int_0^t \xi_1(s)\,d_p(s)\,B_{k,p}(s)\,ds.
\]

Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fk-twostep}
f_k(t,x) = f_k(0,x) - \Gamma_{k,1}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x_0) - \Gamma_{k,2}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x_1).
\end{equation}
The spike shape is determined by $K_{\mu_0}$; all learning dynamics reduce to the \emph{scalar} coefficients $\Gamma_{k,1}(t)$, $\Gamma_{k,2}(t)$. The kernel $K_{\mu_0}$ (NNGP, \cite{jacot2018neural}) is Laplace-like: $K_{\mu_0}(x_p,x_p)=K_0>0$ for $p\in\{0,1\}$ (same by symmetry), and $|K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_1)|= |K_{\mu_0}(-\delta,+\delta)|\le C\,e^{-c\delta}$ for some $C,c>0$, so the off-diagonal is exponentially small in $\delta$.

The full evolution has \emph{two terms} (local plus non-local):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dtfk-full-delta}
\partial_t f_k(t,x_p) = -\xi_1(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x_p,x_p)\,d_p(t)\,B_{k,p+1}(t) + E_p(t),
\end{equation}
where the non-local remainder $E_p(t)$ satisfies $|E_p(t)|\le C'\,e^{-c\delta}$ for some $C',c>0$. We state the theorem for $x_0$ only; the result is symmetrical at $x_1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:finite-support-scalar}).

\begin{theorem}[Two-sided step: log-ratio growth at $x_0$]
\label{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main}
\medskip
\textbf{Assume:} the two-sided step setting above with $r=2$ channels and a 3-layer RF-LR; the dynamics are \eqref{eq:fk-twostep} and the full evolution \eqref{eq:dtfk-full-delta}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Definition (log-ratio criterion).} Let $I \subseteq [0,\infty)$. For $p\in\{0,1\}$, $d_p(t)$ is the residual at $x_p$ and $B_{k,p+1}(t)=B_k(t;x^{(p+1)})$. Define
\[
R_{12}(t,x_0) = \log\frac{|f_1(t,x_0)|}{|f_2(t,x_0)|}.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Suppose (at $x_0$):} $-d_0(t)\ge 0$ on $I$; $B_{1,1}(t)$ has the same sign as $f_1(t,x_0)$; and there exists $\rho_0\in[0,1)$ such that
$|B_{2,1}(t)|\le \rho_0\,\frac{|f_2(t,x_0)|}{|f_1(t,x_0)|}\,|B_{1,1}(t)|$ for all $t\in I$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Then:} For a.e.\ $t\in I$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0) &= (1-\rho_0)\,\xi_1(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_0)\,(-d_0(t))\,\frac{|B_{1,1}(t)|}{|f_1(t,x_0)|} \\
&\quad + \varepsilon_0(t),
\end{aligned}
\]
with $|\varepsilon_0(t)|\le C''\,e^{-c\delta}$ (from $E_p$). Hence $\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0)\ge 0$ whenever the leading term dominates $|\varepsilon_0|$; strict dominance of channel~1 at $x_0$ cannot be lost on $I$ and is amplified whenever $|B_{1,1}|$ is not too small. The result is symmetrical at $x_1$ (channel~2 dominates there).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The proof  is deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:r2-logratio-proof} and only uses \cref{eq:dtfk-full-delta} and the log-ratio calculus at $x_0$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Training in practice.} Our analysis, for tractability, assumes Gaussian inputs and $N \ge 1000$, but the key phenomena---channel feature learning and hierarchical frequency emergence---are observed robustly in practice with finite-width, non-Gaussian data. We have shown that convergence to a global minimizer for three-layer mean-field networks \emph{persists} under low-rank constraints, even without convexity. Recent work has established convergence to a global minimizer for full-rank three-layer networks without convexity, but it remained open whether these guarantees hold under low-rank constraints. \textbf{Our key insight is that the low-rank and random features structure are minimal to maintain the universal approximation property throughout training, which preserves the conditions needed for convergence to a global minimizer.}

\section{Notation and Neuronal Embedding Framework}
\label{app:notation}

In the mean-field framework, neurons are indexed by continuous random variables rather than discrete indices. This \emph{neuronal embedding} approach treats each neuron as a sample from a probability measure, enabling rigorous analysis in the infinite-width limit.

\textbf{Neuronal indices:} We use $C_1 \in \mathcal{C}_1$ and $C_2 \in \mathcal{C}_2$ to denote random variables indexing neurons in the first and second layers, respectively. These are drawn from probability measures $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ on spaces $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$. In the finite-width case, $C_1(j_1)$ and $C_2(j_2)$ correspond to countable or uncountable neuron indices $j_1 \in \{1,\ldots,n_1\}$ and $j_2 \in \{1,\ldots,n_2\}$.

\textbf{Weight functions:} The weights are functions of time and neuronal indices:
\begin{itemize}
\item $w_1(t, C_1, k) \in \mathbb{R}$: weight for channel $k \in \{1,\ldots,r\}$ of neuron $C_1$ in the first layer at time $t$.
\item $w_2(t, C_2) \in \mathbb{R}$: weight for neuron $C_2$ in the second layer at time $t$.
\item $W(t) = (w_1(t, \cdot, \cdot), w_2(t, \cdot))$: the full weight configuration at time $t$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Feature maps and activations:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $L^0(C_1) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$: frozen random feature vector for neuron $C_1$ (drawn i.i.d. from a Gaussian measure); we specify the law of $L^0(C_1)$ when sampling the network.
\item $\varphi_1: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$: activation function for the first layer (e.g., ReLU).
\item $\varphi_2: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$: activation function for the second layer.
\item $H_2(t, c_2; X, W(t)) = \sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\,f_k(t; X, W(t))$: second-layer pre-activation, where $f_k(t; X, W(t)) = \E_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})]$ are the channel-wise partial functions.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Backpropagation signal:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $B_k(t; X) = \E_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2; X, W(t)))\,w_2(t, C_2)]$: channel-$k$ backpropagated signal, aggregating gradient information from the second layer through the mixing matrix $L$, where $k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}$ is the channel index. For ReLU activation, $\relugate$ is the indicator function (ReLU gate).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Loss and learning rates:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $d_L(Z; W(t)) = \partial_{\hat{y}} \mathscr{L}(Y, \hat{y}(X; W(t)))$: loss derivative with respect to the network output, where $Z = (X, Y)$ is a data sample and $\hat{y}(X; W(t))$ is the network output.
\item $\xi_1(t), \xi_2(t) \ge 0$: learning rate schedules for the first and second layers, respectively.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Kernel function:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $K_{\mu_0}(x,X)$: kernel function that measures similarity between input locations $x$ and $X$ with respect to the data distribution $\mu_0$. This kernel appears in the evolution equation for the partial functions $f_k(t,x)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Expectations:} $\E_Z[\cdot]$ denotes expectation over the data distribution, $\E_{C_1}[\cdot]$ over the first-layer neuron measure, and $\E_{C_2}[\cdot]$ over the second-layer neuron measure.

\section{Technical Lemmas and Proofs for Well-Posedness}
\label{app:wellposed-proof}

\subsection{Key Technical Lemma: Bi-Lipschitz Property}
\label{app:bilipschitz}

Before proving well-posedness, we establish a key technical lemma that will be used throughout the proof.

\begin{lemma}[Bi-Lipschitz property of $H_2$]
\label{lem:bilipschitz-H2}
For any $W'=(w_1',w_2')$ and $W''=(w_1'',w_2'')$ satisfying the regularity assumptions, we have:
\[
\big|H_2(t,c_2;X,W')-H_2(t,c_2;X,W'')\big| \le K\,\|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\big[|w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k)|\big],
\]
where $\|L\|_{\infty,1}\equiv \sup_{c_2}\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}|\le rK$ under the entrywise bound.

Moreover, if $\varphi_2$ is ReLU or a Lipschitz activation, then:
\[
\big|\varphi_2(H_2(t,c_2;X,W'))-\varphi_2(H_2(t,c_2;X,W''))\big| \le K\,\|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\big[|w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k)|\big].
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By definition:
\begin{align*}
H_2(t,c_2;X,W')-H_2(t,c_2;X,W'') &= \sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\big(f_k(t;X,W')-f_k(t;X,W'')\big)\\
&= \sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\E_{C_1}\big[(w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k))\,\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})\big].
\end{align*}
Taking absolute values and using boundedness of $\varphi_1$:
\begin{align*}
\big|H_2(t,c_2;X,W')-H_2(t,c_2;X,W'')\big| &\le \sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}|\,\E_{C_1}\big[|w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k)|\big]\,|\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})|\\
&\le K\,\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}|\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\big[|w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k)|\big]\\
&\le K\,\|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\big[|w_1'(t,C_1,k)-w_1''(t,C_1,k)|\big].
\end{align*}
The second part follows from the Lipschitz property of $\varphi_2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Norms and Spaces}
\label{app:wellposed-norms}

We equip the mean-field parameters with several norms. For the low-rank case, we define:

\begin{align*}
\interleave w_1\interleave_t &= \max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|^{50}\right]^{1/50},\\
\interleave w_2\interleave_t &= \E_{C_2}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_2(s,C_2)|^{50}\right]^{1/50},\\
\interleave W\interleave_t &= \max\big(\interleave w_1\interleave_t, \interleave w_2\interleave_t\big).
\end{align*}

We also define the $L^2$-type norms:
\begin{align*}
\|w_1\|_t &= \max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|^2\right]^{1/2},\\
\|w_2\|_t &= \E_{C_2}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_2(s,C_2)|^2\right]^{1/2},\\
\|W\|_t &= \max\big(\|w_1\|_t, \|w_2\|_t\big).
\end{align*}

For the $\psi_2$-type sub-Gaussian norms:
\begin{align*}
\llbracket w_1\rrbracket_{\psi,t} &= \sqrt{50}\,\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|^m\right]^{1/m},\\
\llbracket w_2\rrbracket_{\psi,t} &= \sqrt{50}\,\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\E_{C_2}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_2(s,C_2)|^m\right]^{1/m},\\
\llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,t} &= \max\big(\llbracket w_1\rrbracket_{\psi,t}, \llbracket w_2\rrbracket_{\psi,t}\big).
\end{align*}

The factor $\sqrt{50}$ ensures that $\llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,t}\ge \interleave W\interleave_t$ and hence $\llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,t}\ge \|W\|_t$.

For convenience, we define the random variables:
\begin{align*}
\mathsf{max}_t^w(W) &= \max_{1\le k\le r}\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|,\\
\mathsf{max}_t^{w_2}(W) &= \sup_{s\le t}|w_2(s,C_2)|.
\end{align*}

For two sets of mean-field parameters $W$ and $W'$, we define the distance:
\begin{align}
\|W-W'\|_t &= \max\big(\|w_1-w_1'\|_t, \|w_2-w_2'\|_t\big),\label{eq:def-MF-distance-app}\\
\|w_1-w_1'\|_t &= \max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)-w_1'(s,C_1,k)|^2\right]^{1/2},\nonumber\\
\|w_2-w_2'\|_t &= \E_{C_2}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_2(s,C_2)-w_2'(s,C_2)|^2\right]^{1/2}.\nonumber
\end{align}

\subsection{Solution Operator and Fixed Point Formulation}

Denote by $\mathfrak{W}_T$ the space of mean-field parameters $W$ such that $\|W\|_T<\infty$. Given a terminal time $T\ge 0$ and an initialization $W(0)$, we define the mapping $F$ that associates $W'\in\mathfrak{W}_T$ with:
\begin{align*}
F(W')(t) &= \big\{F_1(W')(t,\cdot,\cdot), F_2(W')(t,\cdot)\big\},
\end{align*}
where
\begin{align*}
F_1(W')(t,c_1,k) &= w_1(0,c_1,k) - \int_0^t \xi_1(s)\,\E_{Z}\!\left[d_L\big(Z;W'(s)\big)\,\varphi_1\!\left(\ip{L^0(c_1)}{X}\right)\,B_k\big(s;X,W'(s)\big)\right]ds,\\
F_2(W')(t,c_2) &= w_2(0,c_2) - \int_0^t \xi_2(s)\,\E_{Z}\!\left[d_L\big(Z;W'(s)\big)\,\varphi_2\!\Big(H_2\big(s,c_2;X,W'(s)\big)\Big)\right]ds,
\end{align*}
and $B_k(s;X,W')=\E_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(s,C_2;X,W'))\,w_2'(s,C_2)]$.

Observe that at initialization $F(W')(0,\cdot,\cdot)=W(0)$, whereas the quantities in the time integrals are computed with respect to $W'$. A solution to the mean-field ODEs on $[0,T]$ is an element of $\mathfrak{W}_T$ satisfying $F(W)=W$. We say that $W$ is a solution on $[0,\infty)$ if its restriction to $[0,T]$ is a solution on $[0,T]$ for all $T>0$.

\subsection{A Priori Bounds}

\begin{lemma}[Weight bounds]
\label{lem:bounds-a-priori}
Under Assumptions~\ref{assump:regularity} and \ref{assump:data}, given an initialization $W(0)$, a solution $W$ to the mean-field ODEs, if it exists, must satisfy that for any $t\in[0,\infty)$:
\[
\interleave W\interleave_t \le K_0(t),
\]
where $K_0(t)$ is a non-decreasing function of the form
\[
K_0(t) = K^{\kappa}(1+t^{\kappa})(1+\interleave W\interleave_0^{\kappa}),
\]
for some constant $\kappa>0$ depending on $K$ and $r$.

A similar result holds for the $\psi_2$ norm. Under the same assumptions, for any $t\in[0,\infty)$, there exists $K_0(t)\ge 1$ of the form
\[
K_0(t) = K^{\kappa}(1+t^{\kappa})(1+\llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,0}^{\kappa}),
\]
such that a solution $W$, if it exists, must satisfy $\interleave W\interleave_t \le \llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,t} \le K_0(t)$ for any $t\in[0,\infty)$. Furthermore, by assuming $\llbracket W\rrbracket_{\psi,0}<\infty$, for any $B\ge 0$:
\[
\Pp\!\left(\max\big(\mathsf{max}_t^w(W), \mathsf{max}_t^{w_2}(W)\big)\ge K_0(t)B\right) \le C e^{-K_1 B^2},
\]
for some universal constants $C, K_1>0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows from Grönwall-type arguments applied to the ODEs \eqref{eq:ode-w1}. The key steps are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Use the boundedness and Lipschitz properties of $\varphi_1$, $\varphi_2$, $\varphi_2'$, and $\partial_2\mathcal{L}$.
\item Control $H_2$ using the low-rank structure: $|H_2|\le \|L\|_{\infty,1}\max_k|f_k|\le rK\max_k\E_{C_1}[|w_1(C_1,k)|]$.
\item Control $B_k$ using the structure: $|B_k|\le K\E_{C_2}[|L_{C_2,k}|\,|w_2|]\le rK^2\E_{C_2}[|w_2|]$.
\item Apply Minkowski's inequality and Grönwall's lemma to obtain polynomial growth in $t$.
\item The sub-Gaussian tail bound follows from the $\psi_2$ control and a union bound.
\end{enumerate}
The constant $\kappa$ depends on $K$ and $r$ through the factor $\|L\|_{\infty,1}\le rK$, but remains polynomial rather than exponential in $r$.
\end{proof}

These a priori bounds lead us to consider the following spaces, given an initialization $W(0)$ and an arbitrary terminal time $T>0$:

\begin{itemize}
\item The space $\mathcal{W}_T$ of mean-field parameters $W'=\{W'(t)\}_{t\le T}$ such that $\interleave W'\interleave_T \le K_0(T)$.

\item The space $\mathcal{W}_T^0\subset\mathcal{W}_T$ of mean-field parameters $W'\in\mathcal{W}_T$ such that:
\begin{align*}
\llbracket W'\rrbracket_{\psi,T} &\le K_0(T),\\
\Pp\!\left(\max\big(\mathsf{max}_T^w(W'), \mathsf{max}_T^{w_2}(W')\big)\ge K_0(T)B\right) &\le C e^{-K_1 B^2} \quad \forall B\ge 0,
\end{align*}
and $W'(0)=W(0)$ (so all elements in $\mathcal{W}_T^0$ share the same initialization).

It is easy to see that $\mathcal{W}_T^0\subset\mathcal{W}_T$ since $\interleave W'\interleave_T \le \llbracket W'\rrbracket_{\psi,T}$.
\end{itemize}

We equip these spaces with the metric $(W',W'')\mapsto \|W'-W''\|_T$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:bounds-a-priori}, any solution $W$ to the mean-field ODEs, if it exists, must belong to $\mathcal{W}_T^0$.

\begin{lemma}[Solution operator maps bounded sets to bounded sets]
\label{lem:invariance-F}
Under Assumptions~\ref{assump:regularity} and \ref{assump:data}, for any $W'\in\mathcal{W}_T^0$, we have $F(W')\in\mathcal{W}_T^0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows the same argument as Lemma~\ref{lem:bounds-a-priori}, using the integral form defining $F$ and the bounded/Lipschitz properties of the drifts. The key is that applying bounded/Lipschitz drifts to $W'$ preserves the moment and tail bounds with constants controlled by $K_0(T)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Difference Estimate}

\begin{lemma}[Solution operator is contractive]
\label{lem:difference-F}
For a given $B\ge 0$, consider two collections of mean-field parameters $W',W''\in\mathcal{W}_T$ such that:
\begin{align*}
\Pp\!\left(\max\big(\mathsf{max}_T^w(W'), \mathsf{max}_T^{w_2}(W')\big)\ge K_0(T)B\right) &\le C e^{-K_1 B^2},\\
\Pp\!\left(\max\big(\mathsf{max}_T^w(W''), \mathsf{max}_T^{w_2}(W'')\big)\ge K_0(T)B\right) &\le C e^{-K_1 B^2}.
\end{align*}
Under Assumptions~\ref{assump:regularity}--\ref{assump:data}, for any $t\le T$:
\[
\|F(W')-F(W'')\|_t \le (KK_0(T))^4 \int_0^t \Big((1+B)\|W'-W''\|_s + \sqrt{2}e^{-K_1 B^2/2}\Big)ds,
\]
where the constant depends on $K$ and $r$ through $\|L\|_{\infty,1}\le rK$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The proof uses a good/bad event decomposition:
\begin{enumerate}
\item On the good event $\{\max(\mathsf{max}_T^w, \mathsf{max}_T^{w_2})\le K_0(T)B\}$, the drifts are Lipschitz in $W$ with constant $(1+B)K_0(T)$.
\item The difference $|H_2(W')-H_2(W'')|$ is controlled using Lemma~\ref{lem:bilipschitz-H2}:
\[
|H_2(W')-H_2(W'')| \le K\|L\|_{\infty,1}\max_k\E_{C_1}[|w_1'(C_1,k)-w_1''(C_1,k)|] \le rK^2\max_k\|w_1'-w_1''\|_t.
\]
\item The difference $|B_k(W')-B_k(W'')|$ is controlled similarly, giving a factor $(1+B)$.
\item The bad event contributes an exponentially small remainder $e^{-K_1 B^2/2}$.
\item Integrating in time and using Minkowski's inequality yields the result.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Complete Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wellposed}}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wellposed}]
We perform a Picard-type iteration argument. Consider an arbitrary finite $T\ge 0$ and $W',W''\in\mathcal{W}_T^0$. From Lemma~\ref{lem:difference-F}:
\begin{align*}
\|F(W')-F(W'')\|_t &\le (KK_0(T))^4\left((1+B)\int_0^t \|W'-W''\|_s ds + T\sqrt{2}e^{-K_1 B^2/2}\right)\\
&\equiv k_1(1+B)\int_0^t \|W'-W''\|_s ds + k_2 e^{-k_3 B^2},
\end{align*}
for any $B>0$, where $k_1=(KK_0(T))^4$, $k_2=(KK_0(T))^4 T\sqrt{2}$, and $k_3=K_1/2$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:invariance-F}, $F$ maps $\mathcal{W}_T^0$ to $\mathcal{W}_T^0$. We can iterate this inequality to obtain:
\begin{align*}
\|F^{(m)}(W')-F^{(m)}(W'')\|_T &\le k_1(1+B)\int_0^T \|F^{(m-1)}(W')-F^{(m-1)}(W'')\|_{T_2} dT_2 + k_2 e^{-k_3 B^2}\\
&\le k_1^2(1+B)^2\int_0^T\int_0^{T_2} \|F^{(m-2)}(W')-F^{(m-2)}(W'')\|_{T_3} \mathbb{I}(T_2\le T) dT_3 dT_2\\
&\quad + k_2\sum_{\ell=1}^2 \frac{(T k_1(1+B))^{\ell-1}}{\ell!} e^{-k_3 B^2}\\
&\quad \ldots\\
&\le \frac{1}{m!} T^m k_1^m (1+B)^m \|W'-W''\|_T + k_2 e^{T k_1(1+B) - k_3 B^2}\\
&\le \frac{1}{m!} T^m k_1^m (1+\sqrt{m})^m \|W'-W''\|_T + k_2 e^{T k_1(1+\sqrt{m}) - k_3 m},
\end{align*}
where we choose $B=\sqrt{m}$ in the last display. Note that since $\interleave W\interleave_0<\infty$, $K_0(T)$ and hence $k_1, k_2$ are finite for finite $T$.

By substituting $W''=F(W')$, we obtain:
\[
\sum_{m=1}^\infty \|F^{(m+1)}(W')-F^{(m)}(W')\|_T = \sum_{m=1}^\infty \|F^{(m)}(W'')-F^{(m)}(W')\|_T < \infty.
\]
Hence as $m\to\infty$, $F^{(m)}(W')$ converges in $\|\cdot\|_T$ to a limit $W\in\mathfrak{W}_T$, which is a fixed point of $F$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:bounds-a-priori}, $W$ belongs to $\mathcal{W}_T^0$.

The uniqueness of the fixed point comes from the above estimate, since if $W'$ and $W''$ are fixed points of $F$, then they are both in $\mathcal{W}_T^0$, and:
\[
\|W'-W''\|_T = \|F^{(m)}(W')-F^{(m)}(W'')\|_T \le \frac{1}{m!} T^m k_1^m (1+\sqrt{m})^m \|W'-W''\|_T + k_2 e^{T k_1(1+\sqrt{m}) - k_3 m},
\]
and one can take $m$ arbitrarily large. This proves that the solution exists and is unique on $t\in[0,T]$. Since $T$ is arbitrary, we have existence and uniqueness of the solution to the mean-field ODEs on the time interval $[0,\infty)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Assumptions}
\label{app:assumptions}

This section contains the complete statement of all assumptions used in our theoretical analysis. These assumptions are referenced in the main text with their names in italics.

\begin{assumption}[Bounded Activations and Mixing]
\label{assump:regularity}
There exists a constant $K\ge 1$ such that:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Activation functions}: $\|\varphi_1\|_\infty\le K$, $\|\varphi_2\|_\infty\le K$, and $\|\varphi_2'\|_\infty\le K$. Moreover, $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2$ are $K$-Lipschitz continuous, and $\varphi_2'$ is non-vanishing (strictly non-zero everywhere).
\item \textbf{Low-rank mixing matrix}: The mixing matrix entries $L_{c_2,k}$ are random variables (e.g., Gaussian) with $\sup_{c_2,k}|L_{c_2,k}|\le K$ almost surely, and $k\mapsto L_{c_2,k}$ is measurable for each $c_2$. This implies $\|L\|_{\infty,1}\equiv \sup_{c_2}\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}|\le rK$ almost surely.
\end{itemize}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Sub-Gaussian Initialization]
\label{assump:init}
The initial weights satisfy sub-Gaussian moment bounds:
\begin{itemize}
\item For the first-layer weights: $\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\!\left[|w_1^0(C_1,k)|^m\right]^{1/m}\le K$ for some $K>0$.
\item For the second-layer weights: $\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\E_{C_2}\!\left[|w_2^0(C_2)|^m\right]^{1/m}\le K$.
\item Equivalently, in terms of $\psi_2$ norms: $\llbracket w_1(0)\rrbracket_{\psi}<\infty$ and $\llbracket w_2(0)\rrbracket_{\psi}<\infty$, where $\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\psi}$ denotes the $\psi_2$ norm controlling moment growth.
\end{itemize}
This ensures that the initial weight distributions have controlled tail behavior, which is essential for the well-posedness argument.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Data Distribution and Loss Regularity]
\label{assump:data}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bounded inputs}: $|X|\le K$ with probability 1, and the feature map satisfies $\|L^0(c_1)\|\le K$ for all $c_1\in\Omega_1$.
\item \textbf{Loss function regularity}: The loss function $\mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y})$ is such that $\partial_2\mathcal{L}(y,\cdot)$ is $K$-bounded and $K$-Lipschitz for all $y$ in the support of $\mathcal{P}$.
\item \textbf{No convexity requirement}: \textbf{Crucially, we do NOT require convexity of the loss function}. This allows our results to apply to non-convex settings like frequency-dependent function approximation, where the loss function is inherently non-convex.
\end{itemize}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Diversity of Random Features]
\label{assump:diversity}
The support of $\rho^1$ (the measure on $\Omega_1$ indexing the first-layer random features) is $\R^d$ (or dense in $\R^d$). This ensures that the random features $\{\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(c_1)}{\cdot}): c_1\in\Omega_1\}$ have dense span in $L^2(\mathcal{P}_X)$, which is essential for the universal approximation property.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Non-Degeneracy]
\label{assump:lr}
To ensure that the limit point $\bar{w}_2$ is non-degenerate (i.e., $\Pp(\bar{w}_2(C_2)\ne 0)>0$), we require one of the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\xi_2(\cdot)\ne 0$ (second layer is trained) and the initial loss satisfies $\mathscr{L}(w_1^0,w_2^0) < \E_Z[\mathcal{L}(Y,\varphi_2(0))]$, then by the gradient flow property, the limit point must have non-zero $w_2$ mass. This condition requires that the initial network performs better than the trivial predictor $\hat y=0$, which is satisfied for most reasonable initializations (e.g., small random weights) with high probability.
\item If $\xi_2(\cdot)=0$ (second layer frozen) and the initialization has non-zero mass, then non-degeneracy is automatic.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[Universal approximation automatically maintained]
\label{thm:uap-automatic}
The learning trajectory automatically maintains the universal approximation property of the function class represented by the first layer's neurons throughout training. Specifically, if $\text{supp}(L^0(C_1)) = \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\varphi_1$ is ReLU (or any non-polynomial activation), then the function class $\{\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(c_1)}{\cdot}): c_1\in\Omega_1\}$ has dense span in $L^2(\mathcal{P}_X)$ throughout training.

This follows from the fact that since $L^0(C_1)$ are frozen random features with full support, and ReLU is non-polynomial, the dense span property is automatically maintained: $\text{ReLU}(af+b)$ for random $a,b$ always forms a dense span. This is the key property that, combined with low-rank structure, enables convergence to a global minimizer even without convexity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{assumption}[Convergence to Limit Point]
\label{assump:convergence}
There exist functions $\bar{w}_1:\Omega_1\times\{1,\dots,r\}\to\R$ and $\bar{w}_2:\Omega_2\to\R$ such that as $t\to\infty$, there exists a coupling $\pi_t$ of $\rho^1\times\rho^2$ and itself such that:
\begin{align}
\int (1+|\bar{w}_2(c_2)|)|\bar{w}_2(c_2)|\max_{1\le k\le r}|\bar{w}_1(c_1,k)|\,|w_1^*(t,c_1',k)-\bar{w}_1(c_1,k)|\,d\pi_t(c_1,c_2,c_1',c_2') &\to 0, \label{eq:conv-w1}\\
\int (1+|\bar{w}_2(c_2)|)|\bar{w}_2(c_2)|\,|w_2^*(t,c_2')-\bar{w}_2(c_2)|\,d\pi_t(c_1,c_2,c_1',c_2') &\to 0, \label{eq:conv-w2}
\end{align}
where $W^*(t)=(w_1^*(t,\cdot,\cdot),w_2^*(t,\cdot))$ is the solution to the mean-field ODEs \eqref{eq:ode-w1}. This assumption ensures that the training dynamics converge to a well-defined limit point in a Wasserstein-like sense.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[On the assumptions]
Universal approximation is automatically maintained (Theorem~\ref{thm:uap-automatic}), which is the crucial difference from previous work. Rather than relying on convexity of the loss, we prove that the function class maintains its approximation power throughout training. The frozen random features in the first layer with full support automatically ensure dense span: since $\text{ReLU}(af+b)$ for random $a,b$ always forms a dense span, this property is automatically maintained rather than assumed, providing a rich fixed basis independent of the low-rank structure in subsequent layers. 

The \emph{Convergence to Limit Point} assumption is typically verified by showing that the loss decreases along trajectories (gradient flow property), establishing compactness of the trajectory set, and using stability arguments such as LaSalle's invariance principle. The low-rank structure enters through the $\max_k$ over $r$ channels in \eqref{eq:conv-w1}, but the overall structure remains similar to the full-rank case.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dataset Descriptions}
\label{app:datasets}

We evaluate our architecture on frequency-dependent function approximation tasks, which provide a controlled setting for studying how low-rank networks learn multi-scale features. The target function is:
\[

\]
where $(f_1, f_2)$ are frequency pairs that determine the complexity of the function. It is symmetric about $x=0$ (both terms use $x^2$, so $f(-x)=f(x)$). The frequency pairs are as in \texttt{experiments/former/SinQuad/benchmark.py} (base pair $(36,12)$; we also use scaled regimes $(72,24)$ and $(144,48)$). This task is inherently non-convex and tests the network's capacity to learn high-frequency components while maintaining convergence to a global minimizer.

We test three frequency regimes that progressively increase in difficulty:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Low frequency} $(36, 12)$: 1000 training samples, 1234 test samples
\item \textbf{Medium frequency} $(72, 24)$: 2000 training samples, 2468 test samples  
\item \textbf{High frequency} $(144, 48)$: 4000 training samples, 4936 test samples
\end{itemize}

The training data is generated using a uniform grid on $[-1, 1]$, while the test set uses random samples from a different distribution to assess generalization. The number of training samples scales linearly with frequency to maintain consistent sampling density relative to the function's Nyquist frequency. This scaling ensures that higher-frequency functions receive proportionally more training data, though the exponential difficulty increase with frequency (see Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) suggests that even this scaling may be insufficient for the highest frequencies.

\subsection{Channel Mixing and Low-Rank Structure}
\label{app:channel-mixing}

% Content from meanfield_lowrank.tex Section 1

The low-rank channel mixing structure enters through:
\[
H_2(t,c_2;X,W)=\sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\,f_k(t;X,W),
\]
where $f_k(t;X,W)=\E_{C_1}[w_1(t,C_1,k)\,\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})]$ are the $r$ partial functions.

Under the entrywise bound $\sup_{c_2,k}|L_{c_2,k}|\le K$, we have:
\[
\|L\|_{\infty,1}\le rK,\qquad \|L\|_{\infty,2}\le \sqrt{r}\,K.
\]

\begin{lemma}[Sub-Gaussian bounds for low-rank sums]
\label{lem:lowrank-psi-app}
Let $(U_k)_{k=1}^r$ be real random variables on a common probability space and $(a_k)_{k=1}^r\in\R^r$ deterministic.
Then for every $m\ge 1$,
\[
\E\Big[\big|\sum_{k=1}^r a_k U_k\big|^m\Big]^{1/m}\le \sum_{k=1}^r |a_k|\,\E[|U_k|^m]^{1/m}
\le \Big(\sum_{k=1}^r |a_k|\Big)\max_{1\le k\le r}\E[|U_k|^m]^{1/m}.
\]
Consequently, any ``$\psi_2$-type'' seminorm defined by $\sup_{m\ge 1}m^{-1/2}\E[|\,\cdot\,|^m]^{1/m}$ satisfies
\[
\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\E\Big[\big|\sum_{k=1}^r a_k U_k\big|^m\Big]^{1/m}
\le \Big(\sum_{k=1}^r |a_k|\Big)\max_{1\le k\le r}\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\E[|U_k|^m]^{1/m}.
\]
If one prefers an $\ell_2$ version, then also $\sum_{k=1}^r |a_k|\le \sqrt{r}\,(\sum_k a_k^2)^{1/2}$ yields a $\sqrt{r}$ factor.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Forward propagation bounds scale with mixing matrix norm]
\label{lem:H2-row-norm-app}
Assume $\varphi_1$ is bounded by $K$.
Then for every $t\ge 0$ and every $c_2\in\Omega_2$,
\[
\sup_{s\le t}\big|H_2(s,c_2;X,W)\big|
\le \Big(\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}|\Big)\max_{1\le k\le r}\sup_{s\le t}|f_k(s;X,W)|
\le \|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\sup_{s\le t}|f_k(s;X,W)|.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\sup_{s\le t}|f_k(s;X,W)|
\le \E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|\,\big|\varphi_1(\ip{L^0(C_1)}{X})\big|\right]
\le K\,\E_{C_1}\!\left[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|\right].
\]
Combining these inequalities and taking moments yields, for every $m\ge 1$,
\[
\E_X\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\sup_{c_2}|H_2(s,c_2;X,W)|^m\Big]^{1/m}
\le K\,\|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\max_{1\le k\le r}\E_{C_1}\Big[\sup_{s\le t}|w_1(s,C_1,k)|^m\Big]^{1/m}.
\]
In particular, in the $\psi_2$-type calibration,
\[
\sqrt{50}\sup_{m\ge 1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,
\E_X\Big[\sup_{s\le t}\sup_{c_2}|H_2(s,c_2;X,W)|^m\Big]^{1/m}
\le K\,\|L\|_{\infty,1}\,\llbracket w_1\rrbracket_{\psi,t}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Partial Functions and Measure-Valued Evolution}
\label{app:partial-functions}

% Content from meanfield_lowrank.tex Section 2-3

The $r$ partial functions $f_k(t,x)$ (channels from layer 1, evaluated at $x$) can be represented as integrals over the untrained feature measure $\mu_0$:
\[
f_k(t,x)=\int_{\R^d}\bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{x})\,\mu_0(d\theta),
\]
where $\bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)$ is the channel-wise conditional average of $w_1$ at feature location $\theta$.

The evolution of the coefficient field $\bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)$ is given by:
\[
\partial_t \bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)
=-\xi_1(t)\,\E_{Z=(X,Y)}\!\left[d_L\big(Z;W(t)\big)\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{X})\,B_k(t;X)\right].
\]

This yields the evolution equation for $f_k$:
\[
\partial_t f_k(t,x)=-\xi_1(t)\,\E_{Z=(X,Y)}\!\left[d_L\big(Z;W(t)\big)\,B_k(t;X)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,X)\right],
\]
where $K_{\mu_0}(x,X)=\int_{\R^d}\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{x})\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{X})\,\mu_0(d\theta)$ is the kernel induced by the untrained feature measure.

This is an $r$-coupled integro-differential system, closed once $B_k(t;X)$ and $d_L(Z;W(t))$ are expressed in terms of the network output, which itself depends on $(f_j)_{j=1}^r$ through $H_2=\sum_{j=1}^r L_{\cdot,j} f_j$.

\subsection{One-Sparse Invariant Manifold}
\label{app:onesparse}

% Content from meanfield_lowrank.tex Section on one-sparse reduction

Under Assumption~\ref{assump:L-symmetric} (random mixing vector with independent, symmetric coordinates), the one-sparse manifold $f(t)=a(t)e_j$ is invariant. This provides a rigorous mechanism for channel specialization even when $L$ has mixed signs.

\begin{assumption}[Random mixing vector, symmetric and independent]
\label{assump:L-symmetric}
The random vector $L_{C_2}=(L_{C_2,1},\dots,L_{C_2,r})$ has independent coordinates, each symmetric about $0$:
$L_{C_2,k}\overset{d}{=}-L_{C_2,k}$ and $\E[L_{C_2,k}^2]<\infty$ for all $k$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}[One-sparse solution structure]
\label{lem:onesparse-app}
Assume the one-spike system with Assumption~\ref{assump:L-symmetric} and fixed-sign residual $d(t)\le 0$. If $f(0)=a_0e_j$ with $a_0>0$, then for all $t\ge 0$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $f(t)=a(t)e_j$ for some $a(t)\ge a_0$
\item For $k\neq j$, $B_k(t)=0$
\item $B_j(t)>0$ and admits explicit representation
\[
B_j(t)=\frac{w_2^0}{2}\E[|L_{C_2,j}|]\;+\;\frac{\E[L_{C_2,j}^2]}{2}\int_0^t \xi_2(s)\,(-d(s))\,a(s)\,ds.
\]
\end{itemize}
Consequently $\partial_t a(t)=-\xi_1(t)\,d(t)\,B_j(t)\ge 0$ on $[0,T]$.
\end{lemma}


\section{Quantitative Approximation by the Mean-Field Limit for Low-Rank Networks}
\label{app:quantitative}

This appendix provides a rigorous quantitative bound on the approximation error between finite-width low-rank networks and their mean-field limit. The key result shows that the approximation error scales as $O(1/\sqrt{n_{\min}} + \sqrt{\epsilon})$ with explicit constants, where $n_{\min}$ is the minimum width across layers and $\epsilon$ is the learning rate step size. This bound holds for any $n_1$ and $n_2$, independent of the data dimension $d$, similar to the full-rank case \cite{nguyen2020global,araujo2019mean}. The bound suggests that widths $n_1, n_2 \approx 1000$ are typically sufficient to observe mean-field behaviors, as empirically validated in \cite{nguyen2019meanfield} for high-dimensional real-world data.

\subsection{Main Result: Approximation by the MF Limit}

\begin{assumption}[Initialization for Low-Rank Networks]
\label{assump:quant-init}
We assume that ${\rm ess\text{-}sup}\max_{1\le k\le r}|w_1^0(C_1,k)| \le K$ and ${\rm ess\text{-}sup}|w_2^0(C_2)| \le K$, where $w_1^0$ and $w_2^0$ are the initial weights as described in the low-rank architecture setup.
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[Finite-width approximation error bound]
\label{thm:quantitative-lowrank}
Given a family $\mathsf{Init}$ of initialization laws and a tuple $\{n_1, n_2\}$ that is in the index set of $\mathsf{Init}$, perform the coupling procedure for the low-rank architecture as described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Fix a terminal time $T \in \epsilon\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}$. Under Assumptions~\ref{assump:regularity}, \ref{assump:quant-init}, and the low-rank structure with mixing matrix $L$ satisfying $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$, for $\epsilon \le 1$, we have with probability at least $1-2\delta$,
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le C_{\exp} \cdot C_{\text{width}} \cdot C_{\log},
\]
where $C_{\exp} = e^{K_T(1+rK)}$, $C_{\text{width}} = 1/\sqrt{n_{\min}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}$, $C_{\log} = \sqrt{\log(3(T+1)n_{\max}^2/\delta + e)}$, with $n_{\min} = \min\{n_1, n_2\}$, $n_{\max} = \max\{n_1, n_2\}$, $K_T = K(1+T^K)$, and the factor $(1+rK)$ accounts for the low-rank structure through $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$.

The exponential factor $e^{K_T(1+rK)}$ reflects the channel feature learning structure of low-rank networks: the $r$ channels evolve independently through the mixing matrix $L$, and the worst-case time complexity becomes exponential in $rK$ when all channels must be learned simultaneously. However, channel feature learning enables a \emph{choice} of which channels to activate, and in practice, only a subset of channels are needed, avoiding the worst-case exponential scaling.
\end{theorem}

The theorem gives a connection between $\mathbf{W}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor)$ (the discrete-time finite-width network) and $W(t)$ (the continuous-time mean-field limit). The key difference from the full-rank case \cite{nguyen2023rigorousframeworkmeanfield} is the multiplicative factor $(1+rK)$ in the exponential constant. In the full-rank setting, the exponential factor is $e^{K_T}$, while our low-rank architecture introduces an additional $(1+rK)$ factor that accounts for the $r$ independent channels evolving through the mixing matrix $L$. This factor reflects the channel feature learning structure: the $r$ channels evolve independently through the mixing matrix $L$, each contributing a factor that reflects the multi-channel nature of the learning dynamics.

\begin{corollary}[Test function approximation quality]
\label{cor:gradient-descent-quality-lowrank}
Under the same setting as Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank}, consider any test function $\psi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ which is $K$-Lipschitz in the second variable uniformly in the first variable (an example of $\psi$ is the loss $\mathscr{L}$). For any $\delta>0$, with probability at least $1-3\delta$,
\[
\sup_{t\leq T}\left|\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi\left(Y,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\left(X;\mathbf{W}\left(\left\lfloor t/\epsilon\right\rfloor \right)\right)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi\left(Y,\hat{y}\left(X;W\left(t\right)\right)\right)\right]\right|\leq e^{2K_T(1+rK)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\min}}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}\right)\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2}{\delta} + e\right),
\]
where $\hat{\mathbf{y}}(X; \mathbf{W})$ and $\hat{y}(X; W)$ denote the outputs of the finite-width and mean-field networks respectively.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The proof follows the same structure as in the full-rank case \cite{nguyen2020global}. Since $\psi$ is $K$-Lipschitz in the second variable, we have:
\[
||\psi(Y, \hat{\mathbf{y}}(X; \mathbf{W})) - \psi(Y, \hat{y}(X; W))| \le K|\hat{\mathbf{y}}(X; \mathbf{W}) - \hat{y}(X; W)|.
\]
The difference $|\hat{\mathbf{y}}(X; \mathbf{W}) - \hat{y}(X; W)|$ can be bounded by the distance $\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W})$ using the low-rank structure of the network. Specifically, for the low-rank architecture, the output difference involves:
\begin{align*}
||\hat{\mathbf{y}}(X; \mathbf{W}) - \hat{y}(X; W)| &\le \sum_{k=1}^r |L_{C_2,k}|\left|\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1}\mathbf{w}_1(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k)\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle)\right.\\
&\quad\left. - \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), X\rangle)]\right| + \text{(similar terms for $w_2$)}\\
&\le rK\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) + K\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) = K(1+rK)\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}),
\end{align*}
where we used $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$. Taking expectation over $Z$ and applying Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank} completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\paragraph*{Comparison with the full-rank Case.}
The main difference between our low-rank result and the full-rank case \cite{nguyen2020global} is the appearance of the factor $(1+rK)$ in the exponential constant. In the full-rank case, the bound is:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le e^{K_T}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\min}}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}\right)\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2}{\delta} + e\right),
\]
whereas in our low-rank case, we have:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le e^{K_T(1+rK)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\min}}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}\right)\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2}{\delta} + e\right).
\]

The factor $(1+rK)$ arises from:
\begin{itemize}
\item The low-rank structure of $H_2$, which involves a sum over $r$ channels: $H_2(t, c_2; X, W) = \sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\,f_k(t; X, W)$.
\item The mixing matrix bounds: $\|L\|_{\infty,1} = \sup_{c_2}\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{c_2,k}| \le rK$.
\item The backpropagated signal $B_k$ which aggregates over $n_2$ neurons with mixing coefficients $L_{C_2,k}$.
\end{itemize}

However, the fundamental structure of the proof remains the same: we still decompose the error into particle coupling and gradient descent discretization, and the scaling with $n_{\min}$ and $\epsilon$ is identical. The low-rank structure only affects the exponential constant, not the polynomial scaling. This suggests that the mean-field approximation quality is preserved under low-rank constraints, with the trade-off being a potentially larger (but still finite) exponential constant.

\begin{remark}[Deterministic mixing matrix and hierarchical learning]
\label{rem:deterministic-L}
The mixing matrix $L$ can be chosen deterministically (e.g., on a grid) rather than randomly, since the proof only requires the boundedness condition $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$. There is no advantage to maximizing the entries of $L$; fixing $L$ deterministically with appropriate structure can enable hierarchical learning, where different channels $k$ specialize to different frequency components or scales through the backpropagated signal $B_k$. This design choice allows for structured learning dynamics while maintaining the same theoretical guarantees.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Particle ODEs for Low-Rank Networks}

We construct auxiliary trajectories, which we call the \textit{particle ODEs} for the low-rank case. These are continuous-time trajectories of finitely many neurons, averaged over the data distribution, adapted to the low-rank structure:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2) &= -\xi_2(t)\mathbb{E}_Z\left[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t))\,\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}(t)))\right],\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k) &= -\xi_1(t)\mathbb{E}_Z\left[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle)\,\tilde{B}_k(t; X, \tilde{W}(t))\right],
\end{align*}
where $j_1 = 1, \ldots, n_1$, $j_2 = 1, \ldots, n_2$, $k = 1, \ldots, r$, $\tilde{W}(t) = (\tilde{w}_1(t, \cdot, \cdot), \tilde{w}_2(t, \cdot))$, and $t \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$. 

The second-layer output for the particle ODEs is:
\[
\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}) = \sum_{k=1}^r L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1} \tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle),
\]
and the backpropagated signal is:
\[
\tilde{B}_k(t; X, \tilde{W}) = \frac{1}{n_2}\sum_{j_2=1}^{n_2} L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}))\,\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2).
\]

We specify the initialization $\tilde{W}(0)$: $\tilde{w}_1(0, j_1, k) = w_1^0(C_1(j_1), k)$ and $\tilde{w}_2(0, j_2) = w_2^0(C_2(j_2))$. That is, it shares the same initialization with the neural network $\mathbf{W}(0)$, and hence is coupled with the neural network and the MF ODEs.

The existence and uniqueness of the solution to the particle ODEs follows from the same proof as in Theorem~\ref{thm:wellposed}, adapted to account for the low-rank structure. We equip $\tilde{W}(t)$ with the norm:
\[
\interleave\tilde{W}\interleave_T = \max\left\{\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}\sup_{t \le T}|\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)|, \max_{j_2 \le n_2}\sup_{t \le T}|\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2)|\right\}.
\]

We define the distance measures:
\begin{align*}
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \tilde{W}) &= \sup\Big\{|w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k) - \tilde{w}_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)|, |w_2(t, C_2(j_2)) - \tilde{w}_2(t, C_2(j_2))|:\\
&\qquad t \le T, j_1 \le n_1, j_2 \le n_2, 1 \le k \le r\Big\},\\
\mathscr{D}_T(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) &= \sup\Big\{|\mathbf{w}_1(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) - \tilde{w}_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)|,\\
&\qquad |\mathbf{w}_2(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) - \tilde{w}_2(t, C_2(j_2))|: t \le T, j_1 \le n_1, j_2 \le n_2, 1 \le k \le r\Big\}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Key Lemmas}

\begin{lemma}[Particle coupling bound]
\label{lem:particle-lowrank}
Under the same setting as Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank}, for any $\delta > 0$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \tilde{W}) \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\min}}}\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2}{\delta} + e\right)e^{K_T(1+rK)},
\]
in which $n_{\min} = \min\{n_1, n_2\}$, $n_{\max} = \max\{n_1, n_2\}$, and $K_T = K(1+T^K)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Gradient descent discretization error]
\label{lem:gradient-lowrank}
Under the same setting as Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank}, for any $\delta > 0$ and $\epsilon \le 1$, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) \le \sqrt{\epsilon\log\left(\frac{2n_1n_2r}{\delta} + e\right)}e^{K_T(1+rK)},
\]
in which $K_T = K(1+T^K)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank}]
Using the triangle inequality:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le \mathscr{D}_T(W, \tilde{W}) + \mathscr{D}_T(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}),
\]
the result follows immediately from Lemmas~\ref{lem:particle-lowrank} and \ref{lem:gradient-lowrank}, noting that the $\log(2n_1n_2r/\delta)$ term can be absorbed into the $\log(3(T+1)n_{\max}^2/\delta + e)$ term up to constants.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:particle-lowrank}}

\begin{proof}
In the following, let $K_t$ denote a generic positive constant that may change from line to line and takes the form $K_t = K(1+t^K)$, such that $K_t \ge 1$ and $K_t \le K_T$ for all $t \le T$. We first note that at initialization, $\mathscr{D}_0(W, \tilde{W}) = 0$. Since $\interleave W\interleave_0 \le K$, we have $\interleave W\interleave_T \le K_T$ by the a priori bounds. Furthermore, it is easy to see that $\interleave\tilde{W}\interleave_0 \le \interleave W\interleave_0 \le K$ almost surely. By the same argument, $\interleave\tilde{W}\interleave_T \le K_T$ almost surely.

We decompose the proof into several steps.

\paragraph*{Step 1: Main bound with low-rank structure.}

Let us define, for brevity, the differences specific to the low-rank architecture:
\begin{align*}
q_2(t, x, j_2, c_2) &= \tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; x, \tilde{W}(t)) - H_2(t, c_2; x, W(t)),\\
q_{B,k}(t, x) &= \tilde{B}_k(t; x, \tilde{W}(t)) - B_k(t; x, W(t)),
\end{align*}
where $H_2(t, c_2; x, W) = \sum_{k=1}^r L_{c_2,k}\,\mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), x\rangle)]$ and $B_k(t; x, W) = \mathbb{E}_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2; x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2)]$.

Consider $t \ge 0$. We first bound the difference in the updates between $W$ and $\tilde{W}$.

\subparagraph*{Bound for $w_2$ and $\tilde{w}_2$:}
By Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity} and the definition of the ODEs:
\begin{align*}
&\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}w_2(t, C_2(j_2))\right|\\
&\quad = \left|\xi_2(t)\mathbb{E}_Z\left[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t))\,\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}(t))) - d_L(Z; W(t))\,\varphi_2(H_2(t, C_2(j_2); X, W(t)))\right]\right|\\
&\quad \le K\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t)) - d_L(Z; W(t))|\,|\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}(t)))|\right]\\
&\quad\quad + K\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|d_L(Z; W(t))|\,|\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}(t))) - \varphi_2(H_2(t, C_2(j_2); X, W(t)))|\right]\\
&\quad \le K_t\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|q_2(t, X, j_2, C_2(j_2))|\right] + K_t\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}),
\end{align*}
where we used that $d_L$ is Lipschitz in $W$, $\varphi_2$ is Lipschitz, and $H_2$ differences are controlled.

\subparagraph*{Bound for $w_1$ and $\tilde{w}_1$:}
For the low-rank case, we have $k = 1, \ldots, r$ channels. By the definition of the ODEs:
\begin{align*}
&\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)\right|\\
&\quad = \left|\xi_1(t)\mathbb{E}_Z\left[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle)\,\tilde{B}_k(t; X, \tilde{W}(t))\right.\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left.\left. - d_L(Z; W(t))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle)\,B_k(t; X, W(t))\right]\right|\\
&\quad \le K\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(t)) - d_L(Z; W(t))|\,|\tilde{B}_k(t; X, \tilde{W}(t))|\right]\\
&\quad\quad + K\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|d_L(Z; W(t))|\,|q_{B,k}(t, X)|\right]\\
&\quad \le K_t\mathbb{E}_Z\left[|q_{B,k}(t, X)|\right] + K_t\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}),
\end{align*}
where the expectation over $j_2$ in $\tilde{B}_k$ will be handled via concentration.

\paragraph*{Step 2: Decomposition of $q_2$ with low-rank structure.}

The key difference from the full-rank case is that $H_2$ involves a sum over $r$ channels. We decompose:
\begin{align*}
||q_2(t, x, j_2, c_2)| &= \left|\sum_{k=1}^r L_{C_2(j_2),k}\left(\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle)\right.\right.\\
&\quad\left.\left. - \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), x\rangle)]\right)\right|\\
&\le \sum_{k=1}^r |L_{C_2(j_2),k}|\left|\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle)\right.\\
&\quad\left. - \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), x\rangle)]\right|.
\end{align*}

We further decompose each term in the sum:
\begin{align*}
&\left|\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle) - \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), x\rangle)]\right|\\
&\quad \le \max_{j_1 \le n_1}\left|\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle) - w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle)\right|\\
&\quad\quad + \left|\frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1}w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle) - \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[w_1(t, C_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1), x\rangle)]\right|\\
&\equiv Q_{2,1,k}(x, j_2) + Q_{2,2,k}(x, j_2).
\end{align*}

Therefore:
\[
||q_2(t, x, j_2, c_2)| \le \sum_{k=1}^r |L_{C_2(j_2),k}|(Q_{2,1,k}(x, j_2) + Q_{2,2,k}(x, j_2)) \le rK\max_{1 \le k \le r}(Q_{2,1,k}(x, j_2) + Q_{2,2,k}(x, j_2)),
\]
where we used $\|L\|_{\infty,1} \le rK$.

\paragraph*{Step 3: Decomposition of $q_{B,k}$ with low-rank structure.}

For the backpropagated signal difference:
\begin{align*}
||q_{B,k}(t, x)| &= \left|\frac{1}{n_2}\sum_{j_2=1}^{n_2} L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; x, \tilde{W}))\,\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2)\right.\\
&\quad\left. - \mathbb{E}_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2; x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2)]\right|.
\end{align*}

We decompose:
\begin{align*}
||q_{B,k}(t, x)| &\le \max_{j_2 \le n_2}\left|L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; x, \tilde{W}))\,\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2)\right.\\
&\quad\left. - L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2(j_2); x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2(j_2))\right|\\
&\quad + \left|\frac{1}{n_2}\sum_{j_2=1}^{n_2} L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2(j_2); x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2(j_2))\right.\\
&\quad\left. - \mathbb{E}_{C_2}[L_{C_2,k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2; x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2)]\right|\\
&\equiv Q_{B,1,k}(x) + Q_{B,2,k}(x).
\end{align*}

For $Q_{B,1,k}$, using Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity} and the fact that $|L_{C_2(j_2),k}| \le K$:
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{E}_Z[Q_{B,1,k}(X)] &\le K\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\left(|\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2) - w_2(t, C_2(j_2))|\right.\\
&\quad\left. + |w_2(t, C_2(j_2))|\,\mathbb{E}_Z[|\tilde{H}_2(t, j_2; X, \tilde{W}) - H_2(t, C_2(j_2); X, W)|]\right)\\
&\le K_t\left(\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}) + \max_{j_2 \le n_2}\mathbb{E}_Z[|q_2(t, X, j_2, C_2(j_2))|]\right).
\end{align*}

\paragraph*{Step 4: Concentration bounds adapted to low-rank.}

For $Q_{2,1,k}$, we have:
\begin{align*}
\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\mathbb{E}_Z[Q_{2,1,k}(X, j_2)] &\le K\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}|\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k) - w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)|\\
&\le K_t\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}).
\end{align*}

For $Q_{2,2,k}$, we apply concentration. Let us write:
\[
Z_{1,k}(x, c_1) = w_1(t, c_1, k)\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(c_1), x\rangle).
\]
Recall that $\{C_1(j_1)\}_{j_1=1}^{n_1}$ are i.i.d. We have:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1,k}(X, C_1(j_1))|X] = \mathbb{E}_{C_1}[Z_{1,k}(X, C_1)],
\]
and $\{Z_{1,k}(X, C_1(j_1))\}_{j_1=1}^{n_1}$ are independent conditional on $X$. By Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}, $|Z_{1,k}(X, C_1(j_1))| \le K_t$ almost surely. Then by concentration inequalities:
\[
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}_Z[Q_{2,2,k}(X, j_2)] \ge K_t\gamma_{2,k}\right) \le \frac{1}{\gamma_{2,k}}\exp\left(-\frac{n_1\gamma_{2,k}^2}{K_t}\right).
\]

For $Q_{B,2,k}$, we note that almost surely:
\[
||L_{C_2(j_2),k}\,\varphi_2'(H_2(t, C_2(j_2); x, W))\,w_2(t, C_2(j_2))| \le K|L_{C_2(j_2),k}| \le K^2,
\]
by Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}. Then by concentration inequalities:
\[
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{E}_Z[Q_{B,2,k}(X)] \ge K_t\gamma_{1,k}\right) \le \frac{1}{\gamma_{1,k}}\exp\left(-\frac{n_2\gamma_{1,k}^2}{K_t}\right).
\]

\paragraph*{Step 5: Combining bounds with union over $k$.}

Taking a union bound over $k = 1, \ldots, r$ channels, we obtain that for any $\gamma_1, \gamma_2 > 0$ and $t \ge 0$, the event:
\begin{align*}
&\max\Bigg\{\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\mathbb{E}_Z[|q_2(t, X, j_2, C_2(j_2))|],\\
&\qquad\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}\mathbb{E}_Z[|q_{B,k}(t, X)|]\Bigg\}\\
&\quad \le K_t(1+rK)(\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}) + \gamma_1 + \gamma_2),
\end{align*}
holds with probability at least:
\[
1 - \frac{n_1r}{\gamma_1}\exp\left(-\frac{n_2\gamma_1^2}{K_t(1+rK)^2}\right) - \frac{n_2r}{\gamma_2}\exp\left(-\frac{n_1\gamma_2^2}{K_t(1+rK)^2}\right).
\]

\paragraph*{Step 6: Gronwall argument.}

Combining the bounds and taking a union bound over a discrete time grid $t \in \{0, \xi, 2\xi, \ldots, \lfloor T/\xi\rfloor\xi\}$ for some $\xi \in (0,1)$, we obtain:
\[
\max\left\{\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_2(t, j_2) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}w_2(t, C_2(j_2))\right|,\right.
\]
\[
\left.\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}w_1(t, C_1(j_1), k)\right|\right\}
\]
\[
\le K_T(1+rK)(\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}) + \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 + \xi), \qquad \forall t \in [0, T],
\]
with probability at least:
\[
1 - \frac{T+1}{\xi}\left[\frac{n_1r}{\gamma_1}\exp\left(-\frac{n_2\gamma_1^2}{K_T(1+rK)^2}\right) + \frac{n_2r}{\gamma_2}\exp\left(-\frac{n_1\gamma_2^2}{K_T(1+rK)^2}\right)\right].
\]

The above event implies:
\[
\mathscr{D}_t(W, \tilde{W}) \le K_T(1+rK)\int_0^t(\mathscr{D}_s(W, \tilde{W}) + \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 + \xi)ds,
\]
and hence by Gronwall's lemma and the fact $\mathscr{D}_0(W, \tilde{W}) = 0$:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \tilde{W}) \le (\gamma_1 + \gamma_2 + \xi)e^{K_T(1+rK)}.
\]

The result follows from choosing:
\begin{align*}
\xi &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\max}}}, \\
\gamma_1 &= \frac{K_T(1+rK)}{\sqrt{n_2}}\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2r}{\delta} + e\right), \\
\gamma_2 &= \frac{K_T(1+rK)}{\sqrt{n_1}}\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2r}{\delta} + e\right).
\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:gradient-lowrank}}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows the same structure as the full-rank case \cite{nguyen2020global}, with careful adaptations for the low-rank structure. The key difference is that we need to account for the $r$ channels in $w_1$ and the mixing matrix $L$ in all bounds.

We consider $t \le T$ for a given terminal time $T \in \epsilon\mathbb{N}_{\ge 0}$. We reuse the notation $K_t$ from the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:particle-lowrank}. Note that $K_t \le K_T$ for all $t \le T$. We also note that at initialization, $\mathscr{D}_0(\mathbf{W}, \tilde{W}) = 0$.

For brevity, let us define quantities that relate to the difference in the gradient updates between $\mathbf{W}$ and $\tilde{W}$:
\begin{align*}
q_2(k, z, \tilde{z}, j_2) &= d_L(z; \mathbf{W}(k))\,\varphi_2(\mathbf{H}_2(k, j_2; x, \mathbf{W}(k)))\\
&\quad - d_L(\tilde{z}; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(k\epsilon, j_2; \tilde{x}, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))),\\
\textcolor{red}{r_2(k, z, j_2)} &= \xi_2(k\epsilon)d_L(z; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(k\epsilon, j_2; x, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon)))\\
&\quad - \xi_2(k\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_Z[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_2(\tilde{H}_2(k\epsilon, j_2; X, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon)))],\\
q_1(k, z, \tilde{z}, j_1, k') &= d_L(z; \mathbf{W}(k))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle)\,\mathbf{B}_{k'}(k; x, \mathbf{W}(k))\\
&\quad - d_L(\tilde{z}; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), \tilde{x}\rangle)\,\tilde{B}_{k'}(k\epsilon; \tilde{x}, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon)),\\
\textcolor{red}{r_1(k, z, j_1, k')} &= \xi_1(k\epsilon)d_L(z; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), x\rangle)\,\tilde{B}_{k'}(k\epsilon; x, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\\
&\quad - \xi_1(k\epsilon)\mathbb{E}_Z[d_L(Z; \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))\,\varphi_1(\langle L^0(C_1(j_1)), X\rangle)\,\tilde{B}_{k'}(k\epsilon; X, \tilde{W}(k\epsilon))],
\end{align*}
where $\mathbf{B}_{k'}(k; x, \mathbf{W}) = \frac{1}{n_2}\sum_{j_2=1}^{n_2} L_{C_2(j_2),k'}\,\varphi_2'(\mathbf{H}_2(k, j_2; x, \mathbf{W}))\,\mathbf{w}_2(k, j_2)$.

By time-interpolation estimates (similar to Claim 1 in the full-rank case) and Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity}, we have:
\begin{align*}
||\mathbf{w}_2(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) - \tilde{w}_2(t, j_2)| &\le K\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\left[Q_{2,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) + Q_{2,2}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2)\right] + tK_t\epsilon,\\
||\mathbf{w}_1(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) - \tilde{w}_1(t, j_1, k)| &\le K\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}\left[Q_{1,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) + Q_{1,2}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k)\right] + tK_t\epsilon,
\end{align*}
where:
\begin{align*}
Q_{2,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) &= \epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}|q_2(\ell, z(\ell), z(\ell), j_2)|,\\
Q_{2,2}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) &= \left|\epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}\textcolor{red}{r_2(\ell, z(\ell), j_2)}\right|,\\
Q_{1,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) &= \epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}|q_1(\ell, z(\ell), z(\ell), j_1, k)|,\\
Q_{1,2}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) &= \left|\epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}\textcolor{red}{r_1(\ell, z(\ell), j_1, k)}\right|.
\end{align*}

\paragraph*{Bounding the terms:}

For $Q_{2,1}$, using Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity} and the low-rank structure:
\begin{align*}
||q_2(\ell, z(\ell), z(\ell), j_2)| &\le K|d_L(z(\ell); \mathbf{W}(\ell)) - d_L(z(\ell); \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\\
&\quad + K|d_L(z(\ell); \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\,|\mathbf{H}_2(\ell, j_2; x(\ell), \mathbf{W}(\ell)) - \tilde{H}_2(\ell\epsilon, j_2; x(\ell), \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\\
&\le K_t\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) + K_t\sum_{k=1}^r |L_{C_2(j_2),k}|\max_{j_1 \le n_1}|\mathbf{w}_1(\ell, j_1, k) - \tilde{w}_1(\ell\epsilon, j_1, k)|\\
&\le K_t(1+rK)\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}),
\end{align*}
which yields:
\[
\max_{j_2 \le n_2}Q_{2,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_2) \le K_t(1+rK)\epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}).
\]

For $Q_{1,1}$, similarly:
\begin{align*}
||q_1(\ell, z(\ell), z(\ell), j_1, k)| &\le K|d_L(z(\ell); \mathbf{W}(\ell)) - d_L(z(\ell); \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\,|\mathbf{B}_k(\ell; x(\ell), \mathbf{W}(\ell))|\\
&\quad + K|d_L(z(\ell); \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\,|\mathbf{B}_k(\ell; x(\ell), \mathbf{W}(\ell)) - \tilde{B}_k(\ell\epsilon; x(\ell), \tilde{W}(\ell\epsilon))|\\
&\le K_t(1+rK)\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}),
\end{align*}
which yields:
\[
\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}Q_{1,1}(\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor, j_1, k) \le K_t(1+rK)\epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}).
\]

For $Q_{2,2}$ and $Q_{1,2}$, we use martingale concentration. The martingale differences are bounded: $\textcolor{red}{|r_2(\ell, z(\ell), j_2)| \le K_t}$ and $\textcolor{red}{|r_1(\ell, z(\ell), j_1, k)| \le K_t(1+rK)}$ almost surely by Assumption~\ref{assump:regularity} and the low-rank structure. Then by martingale concentration inequalities:
\[
\textcolor{red}{\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j_2 \le n_2}\max_{\ell \in \{0,1,\ldots,T/\epsilon\}}Q_{2,2}(\ell, j_2) \ge \xi\right) \le 2n_2\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{K_T(T+1)\epsilon}\right),}
\]
\[
\textcolor{red}{\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j_1 \le n_1, 1 \le k \le r}\max_{\ell \in \{0,1,\ldots,T/\epsilon\}}Q_{1,2}(\ell, j_1, k) \ge \xi\right) \le 2n_1r\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{K_T(1+rK)^2(T+1)\epsilon}\right).}
\]

\paragraph*{Putting everything together:}

All the above results give us:
\[
\mathscr{D}_{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) \le K_T(1+rK)\epsilon\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor t/\epsilon\rfloor - 1}\mathscr{D}_{\ell\epsilon}(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) + \xi + TK_T\epsilon, \qquad \forall t \le T,
\]
which holds with probability at least:
\[
1 - 2n_1r\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{K_T(1+rK)^2(T+1)\epsilon}\right) - 2n_2\exp\left(-\frac{\xi^2}{K_T(T+1)\epsilon}\right).
\]

The above event implies, by Gronwall's lemma:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(\tilde{W}, \mathbf{W}) \le (\xi + \epsilon)e^{K_T(1+rK)}.
\]

Choosing $\xi = K_T(1+rK)\sqrt{(T+1)\epsilon\log(2n_1n_2r/\delta)}$ completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Discussion: Width Requirement and Exponential Factor}

The bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:quantitative-lowrank} shows that the approximation error scales as:
\[
\mathscr{D}_T(W, \mathbf{W}) \le e^{K_T(1+rK)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{\min}}} + \sqrt{\epsilon}\right)\log^{1/2}\left(\frac{3(T+1)n_{\max}^2}{\delta} + e\right).
\]

For typical values $T \le 10$, $K \approx 1$, $r \le 100$, and $\epsilon \approx 0.001$, we have $K_T(1+rK) \le 1000$ (roughly), so $e^{K_T(1+rK)} \le e^{1000}$, which is an extremely large exponential factor. This reflects the worst-case scenario where all $r$ channels must be learned simultaneously. However, in practice, channel specialization enables a more favorable learning regime: channels progressively capture different frequency components, avoiding the worst-case exponential scaling. The actual convergence rate is determined by the favorable loss landscape structure (see Section~\ref{subsec:training-practice}) rather than this worst-case bound.

The key qualitative insight is that the error \emph{decreases} as $n_{\min}$ increases, with rate $O(1/\sqrt{n_{\min}})$ independent of data dimension $d$. The exponential factor $e^{K_T(1+rK)}$ reflects the multi-channel structure but does not dominate in practice due to channel specialization.

Empirical validation in \cite{nguyen2019meanfield} confirms that networks with width $\approx 1000$ trained on high-dimensional real-world data (e.g., $d \approx 1000$) exhibit mean-field behaviors, supporting our theoretical prediction that the required width is independent of $d$.

\section{Finite-support reduction and scalar ODEs}
\label{app:finite-support-scalar}

This appendix collects the general setup that justifies the \emph{two-sided step} toy model and the reduction of spike learning to scalar ODEs. The $w_1$ evolution is linear in feature space once residuals and backprop signals are fixed; for finitely-supported data one can solve $f_k$ exactly as a superposition of kernel bumps.

\paragraph{Origin of $K_{\mu_0}$.}
The kernel $K_{\mu_0}$ arises from the mean-field $\partial_t f_k$ evolution. One has $f_k(t,x)=\int \bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{x})\,\mu_0(d\theta)$ with $\mu_0=(w_0)_\#\rho^1$ the pushforward of the untrained first-layer feature map. The $w_1$-ODE yields $\partial_t \bar w_{1,k}(t,\theta)=-\xi_1(t)\,\E_{Z=(X,Y)}[d_L\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{X})\,B_k(t;X)]$; differentiating under the integral gives $\partial_t f_k(t,x)=-\xi_1(t)\,\E_Z[d_L\,B_k(t;X)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,X)]$ with
\[
K_{\mu_0}(x,x')\equiv \int_{\R^d}\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{x})\,\varphi_1(\ip{\theta}{x'})\,\mu_0(d\theta).
\]
For ReLU $\varphi_1$ and $\mu_0$ the pushforward of i.i.d. Gaussian or uniform-on-sphere first-layer weights, $K_{\mu_0}$ coincides with the first-layer NNGP kernel \cite{jacot2018neural}. We do not assume $K_{\mu_0}$ is diagonal-dominant on a full Gram matrix; for the two-point support $\{x_0,x_1\}$ we only use that $|K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_1)|=O(e^{-c\delta})$ (Laplace-like decay for separated points), which holds for such NNGP kernels in 1D or under suitable geometry.

\paragraph{Finite-support data.}
Assume the input marginal is supported on $x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(m)}$ with $\mathbb{P}(X=x^{(p)})=\pi_p$ and targets $y^{(p)}$. Set $d_p(t)=d_L((x^{(p)},y^{(p)});W(t))$ and $B_{k,p}(t)=B_k(t;x^{(p)})$. The mean-field $w_1$-PDE integrated in time yields
\[
\Gamma_{k,p}(t)=\int_0^t \xi_1(s)\,\pi_p\,d_p(s)\,B_{k,p}(s)\,ds,
\]
and
\[
f_k(t,x)=f_k(0,x)-\sum_{p=1}^m \Gamma_{k,p}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x^{(p)}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,r.
\]
The spike shape is determined by $K_{\mu_0}$; all learning dynamics reduce to the scalar coefficients $\Gamma_{k,p}(t)$.

\paragraph{One-spike reduction.}
If $m=1$ with $x^{(1)}=x_\star$, then $f_k(t,x)=f_k(0,x)-\Gamma_{k,1}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x_\star)$. For $f_k(0,\cdot)\equiv 0$, $f_k(t,\cdot)$ is exactly a kernel bump at $x_\star$ and spike learning reduces to solving $\Gamma_{k,1}(t)$.

\paragraph{Two-sided step ($m=2$).}
Take $x^{(1)}=x_0=-\delta$, $x^{(2)}=x_1=+\delta$ ($\delta>0$), $y^{(1)}=+A$, $y^{(2)}=-A$. Then
\[
f_k(t,x)=f_k(0,x)-\Gamma_{k,1}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x_0)-\Gamma_{k,2}(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x,x_1).
\]
The kernel $K_{\mu_0}$ (NNGP, \cite{jacot2018neural}) is Laplace-like: $K_{\mu_0}(x_p,x_p)=K_0>0$ for $p\in\{0,1\}$, and $|K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_1)|=|K_{\mu_0}(-\delta,+\delta)|\le C\,e^{-c\delta}$ for some $C,c>0$. The full evolution is
\[
\partial_t f_k(t,x_p)=-\xi_1(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x_p,x_p)\,d_p(t)\,B_{k,p+1}(t)+E_p(t),
\]
with $|E_p(t)|\le C'\,e^{-c\delta}$. This is the setting of Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main}.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:r2-logratio-criterion-main} (two-sided step, $x_0$)}
\label{app:r2-logratio-proof}

We prove the theorem at $x_0$; the argument at $x_1$ is symmetrical.

\paragraph{At $x_0$.}
By the log-ratio derivative identity at $x_0$,
\[
\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0)=\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_1)\,\partial_t f_1}{|f_1|}-\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_2)\,\partial_t f_2}{|f_2|}.
\]
Insert the full evolution \eqref{eq:dtfk-full-delta}: $\partial_t f_k(t,x_0)=-\xi_1\,K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_0)\,d_0\,B_{k,1}+E_0(t)$ with $|E_0(t)|\le C'\,e^{-c\delta}$. Then
\[
\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0)=\xi_1(t)\,K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_0)\,(-d_0(t))\Big(
\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_1)B_{1,1}}{|f_1|}-\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_2)B_{2,1}}{|f_2|}\Big)
+ \varepsilon_0(t),\qquad |\varepsilon_0(t)|\le C''\,e^{-c\delta},
\]
where $\varepsilon_0(t)=E_0(t)\,\big(\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_1)}{|f_1|}-\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_2)}{|f_2|}\big)$ inherits the bound from $E_0$. Use $-d_0\ge 0$. The sign condition gives $\mathrm{sign}(f_1)B_{1,1}=|B_{1,1}|$; $-\mathrm{sign}(f_2)B_{2,1}\ge -|B_{2,1}|$. The dominance inequality at $x_0$ is $|B_{2,1}|\le \rho_0\,\frac{|f_2|}{|f_1|}\,|B_{1,1}|$, hence
\[
\frac{|B_{1,1}|}{|f_1|}-\frac{\mathrm{sign}(f_2)B_{2,1}}{|f_2|}
\ge \frac{|B_{1,1}|}{|f_1|}-\frac{|B_{2,1}|}{|f_2|}
\ge (1-\rho_0)\,\frac{|B_{1,1}|}{|f_1|},
\]
so $\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0)= (1-\rho_0)\,\xi_1\,K_{\mu_0}(x_0,x_0)\,(-d_0)\,\frac{|B_{1,1}|}{|f_1|}+\varepsilon_0(t)$; the leading term is $\ge 0$, hence $\partial_t R_{12}(t,x_0)\ge 0$ whenever it dominates $|\varepsilon_0|$.

\paragraph{At $x_1$.}
The same argument applies by symmetry (indices 1$\leftrightarrow$2, $x_0\leftrightarrow x_1$): channel~2 dominates at $x_1$ and $\partial_t R_{21}(t,x_1)\ge 0$.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
The log-ratio $R_{12}$ at $x_0$ is non-decreasing on $I$; strict dominance of channel~1 at $x_0$ cannot be lost and is amplified whenever $|B_{1,1}|$ is not too small. By symmetry, channel~2 dominates at $x_1$.
